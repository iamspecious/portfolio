1. Who this is for, and what it isn't#

Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 9 November 2026.

Who it's for: everyone. This section tells you where to start.

If you've just hired someone, or you're about to, and you're expecting them to be up and running in a couple of weeks because they've done the job before, this is for you. I'm writing for founders making their first hire, People and ops leads running the process, managers with a new starter on Monday, and the new starter themselves.

Here's the pattern I see most. A company spends weeks and real money finding the right person. Then it hands them a laptop, a wiki and a vague "shout if you need anything", and waits. When they're slow to get going, or quiet, or they start doing things slightly wrong, the conversation turns to whether they were a bad hire. Sometimes they were. A lot of the time nobody ever told them how things actually work here.

Experience doesn't come with a map of your company. Every new hire walks into new people, new systems, new customers and a pile of unwritten rules. Nobody can read minds, however senior they are. So this playbook starts from one idea: the organisation's job is to make the unwritten stuff written, and to do it before it costs anyone their job.

I'm neurodivergent, and I've spent more of my working life than I'd like guessing at things a decent onboarding would have just told me. That's shaped this playbook. Being explicit helps everyone settle in faster, and for some people it's the difference between settling in and not. So you won't find a separate chapter on adjustments here. Being explicit runs through the whole thing.

Does any of this sound familiar?#

These are illustrative, not real companies, but you'll probably recognise at least one. They come back as examples later on.

  • You're the founder of a 12-person company and you're about to make your first hire who isn't a friend. The laptop's ordered. That's the plan so far.
  • You're the only People person at a 150-person company. The last two senior hires left inside six months, and the leadership team has started calling them bad hires.
  • You're a manager with six reports, and a seventh starts on Monday. There's a 40-page wiki and you don't have a free afternoon until the end of the month.
  • You're the new hire. You're three weeks in. You're keeping quiet because you're not sure what you're allowed to ask, and your manager has started mentioning probation.

Each of those needs something different, and section 2 onwards is how to give it to them.

Find your way in#

  • Founders and executives: start with section 2, on why this deserves money and attention, then skim section 9 for what to measure.
  • People, HR and ops leads: sections 3 to 6 are the build. Section 9 is how you'll know it's working. Section 10 has the templates.
  • Line managers: sections 5 and 6 are what you'll do week to week. Sections 7 and 8 are the tricky conversations. Section 10 has the checklists.
  • New hires: section 11 is written for you. It explains why your first few months look the way they do.

What this isn't#

It isn't a list of tools or vendors. Everything here works with a shared doc and a calendar.

It isn't legal advice. Contracts, right-to-work checks, probation rules and data protection all depend on where you are, so check local law before you change any of them.

It isn't a three-month course of documents and videos either. Bauer's guide and the research on how newcomers learn suggest that kind of passive material teaches people very little about how a place really works, and section 3 explains why.

And it isn't the only way to do this. When I give you a specific, like a 90-day buddy or a check-in at six months, it's an example of a choice you'll make, not a rule. There's next to no research on the right number of days for anything in onboarding, and I'll say so every time it matters.

It doesn't cover hiring, performance reviews, pay or long-term career paths. Those are other volumes. Volume 1 covers job architecture and levelling, and it shows up here wherever onboarding is the first time someone sees what their level actually expects of them.

How sure am I?#

Everything in here is one of three kinds, and I've tried to make it obvious which without tagging every line. If there's a link, there's a source behind it, and the text says whether that's research or someone's published practice. If it's my view, I'll say so in plain words. A lot of what gets written about onboarding is a vendor's survey dressed up as research, and I'd rather you know which is which.

2. Why bother: what thin onboarding actually costs#

Section last reviewed 9 November 2026.

Who it's for: founders and executives, and anyone who has to make the case to them.

You've already paid to find this person. Onboarding is how you protect that spend, and most of what it costs is attention, not money. That's the whole argument of this section, and the rest is the evidence for it, including where the evidence runs out.

Experience doesn't travel as well as you'd think#

The instinct is that an experienced hire needs less help. The research doesn't back that up.

A meta-analysis of 81 samples found that the experience people bring with them barely predicts how well they'll perform in a new organisation, and doesn't predict whether they'll stay at all (Van Iddekinge et al., 2019; publisher). The one exception was that experience helps a little more right at the start of a new job. So it gives people a head start. It doesn't give them a map.

The same shows up at the top end. A study of star stock analysts found that those who moved firm saw their performance drop, and stay down, for at least five years. The drop was smaller, or didn't happen, when they moved with their team or to a firm with better support around them (Groysberg, Lee and Nanda, 2008). A lot of what made them good lived in their old relationships and systems, not just in their heads.

That's what onboarding is for. It rebuilds the relationships and know-how a new hire left behind, in your organisation, as fast as you can.

What an early exit costs you#

The most careful figure I've found comes from a review of 30 case studies, which put the typical cost of replacing someone at about a fifth of their annual salary. Most estimates sat between 10 and 30 percent. For executives it ran as high as 213 percent (Boushey and Glynn, 2012). It's US data from older studies, so treat it as a range, not a price.

Your own numbers will tell you more. Fill this in for one recent early exit, roughly. It takes ten minutes.

The gap while the role was empty Work delayed, dropped or overtime paid

Most people who do this for the first time are surprised by the third line. It's the one nobody budgets for.

Bad hire or bad onboarding?#

When a new hire struggles, the easiest explanation is that they were the wrong person. Sometimes that's true. But the research keeps pointing at what happens around them.

In one longitudinal study of 205 newcomers, the new hire's own question-asking wasn't a strong predictor of how well they settled. What their manager did was (Bauer and Green, 1998). Another study followed 264 newcomers through 14 rounds of surveys. Support from managers and colleagues dropped off within the first 90 days, and a manager actively undermining a new hire was uniquely linked to them leaving (Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2013; publisher).

What nobody has measured is how many "bad hires" were really onboarding failures. I've looked. So instead of a statistic, here are four questions to answer before you decide anyone was the wrong hire:

  1. Did they know what good looked like in their role, in writing?
  2. Did they know who to ask, and did asking feel safe?
  3. Did their manager meet them regularly through the first few months, not just the first week?
  4. Was the thing they kept getting wrong written down anywhere?

If any answer is no, fix that first. Section 8 has the full version.

Small changes, real effects#

The strongest evidence that onboarding changes outcomes comes from a 2024 meta-analysis of 83 field experiments. On average, newcomers who went through an onboarding or socialisation programme had 1.46 times the odds of staying, compared with those who didn't. The effect was bigger when programmes helped people work out what effective work looks like, encouraged them to be proactive and helped them connect with colleagues, and when they were delivered in person and spread out over time rather than all at once (Liu et al., 2024). The experiments covered workplaces as well as settings like universities and the military, and the effect held across them.

The most vivid single example is also the most encouraging, because the change was tiny. In a field experiment with around 600 new hires at a call centre in India, changing one hour of the first day, so it focused on each person's own strengths rather than on the company, made new hires less likely to quit in their first months and improved what customers said about them (Cable, Gino and Staats, 2013; publisher).

On the practitioner side, Google's former head of people, Laszlo Bock, writes in his book Work Rules! (2015) that a short email to managers the day before a new hire starts, with five things to do, got new hires up to speed noticeably faster. That's his account of internal data that hasn't been published, so it's an example, not evidence. The five things were simple: talk about the role, assign a buddy, help them build a network, check in monthly for six months, and keep the conversation open.

Neither of those cost much money. They cost someone's attention at the right moment.

How the gains feed each other#

The financial, operational and people sides of this aren't separate arguments. Fewer early exits means less spent on recruiting again. People who get up to speed faster take less of their manager's time and stop leaning on the team sooner. A team that isn't carrying a struggling newcomer has more room to help the next one. And a new hire who was set up properly tends to set up the next person properly too.

That's my view of how it compounds. I can't point you to a study that adds those effects up, and I'd be wary of anyone who claims they can.

An example#

This is illustrative, not a real company.

Remember the People person from section 1? 150 people, two senior hires gone inside six months, and the leadership team calling them bad hires.

The cost figures are rougher: old, US-based and varied. And nobody has counted how many bad hires were onboarding failures, so that part is my view, built on what the research does show.

What would change my mind: good evidence that experienced hires with very little onboarding settle in, contribute and stay just as well as experienced hires who get a structured start. If you've got that data, I want to see it.

3. What onboarding is actually for#

Section last reviewed 9 November 2026.

Who it's for: People and ops leads, line managers, and new hires who want to know why things are done this way.

Onboarding isn't the first week's admin. It's whatever helps a new person know what's expected of them, believe they can do it, feel accepted, understand how things really work here, and feel they fit. Get those right and the outcomes everyone cares about tend to follow. Get them wrong and no amount of paperwork makes up for it.

The five things a new hire needs#

Researchers call this newcomer adjustment, and it's one of the better-studied areas in this whole field. A meta-analysis of 70 groups of new starters found that three things sat between what an organisation did and the outcomes it wanted: knowing what's expected, confidence in doing it, and feeling accepted by colleagues. Those three were linked to satisfaction, commitment, performance, wanting to stay and actually staying (Bauer et al., 2007; publisher). A much larger update of that work, covering 183 studies, added getting a grip on the tasks themselves and a sense of fit, highlighted how much feeling accepted matters, and added wellbeing as an outcome worth caring about (Bauer et al., 2025). Bauer's practical guide for the SHRM Foundation adds a fifth: understanding the culture, meaning the politics, the goals and the local language (Bauer, 2010).

What they need What it looks like when it's missing Mostly covered in
Know what's expected Busy on the wrong things, or waiting to be told The role pillar, section 4
Believe they can do it Over-checking, or avoiding the hard parts Early real work with feedback, section 5
Feel accepted Doesn't know who to go to, isn't included in team invitations, or says they don't feel part of the team Buddy and team, section 6
Understand how things work here Doing things the "right" way that's wrong here Business, operations and culture pillars, section 4
Feel they fit Quietly job hunting by month four Growth pillar and the six-month check, sections 4 and 5

Every pillar and every practice later in this playbook is there to move one of those five. If something in your onboarding doesn't, it's admin, and that's fine, but don't mistake it for onboarding.

Why being explicit matters#

There's good evidence that structure helps. A meta-analysis of around 30 studies found that more structured onboarding, such as steps in a set order and experienced colleagues as role models, went with less confusion about the role, less conflict between expectations, and fewer people planning to leave. The social parts, having experienced colleagues as role models and affirming who the newcomer already is, were the strongest predictors (Saks, Uggerslev and Fassina, 2007, abstract; publisher). The same review found these links were stronger for recent graduates than for other newcomers. I don't read that as "experienced hires need less". I read it as "experienced hires need different": less on how to do the job, more on how this place does it.

The other half of the case is about who pays when things are left unsaid. Asking questions has a social cost for a new starter. Researchers have long pointed out that newcomers worry about looking bothersome or out of their depth, and a study of 394 newcomers asking their supervisors for information found that worry was real. The same study found that asking also made newcomers more confident they were coming across as committed, and that favourable feedback from the supervisor eased the worry and strengthened that confidence (Deng et al., 2024, published online in 2023). So how a manager responds to questions matters as much as whether they're asked. And the people who ask most tend to be the more extraverted and open (Wanberg and Kammeyer-Mueller, 2000; publisher). So an onboarding that relies on the new hire chasing information quietly favours some personalities over others.

That's where the neurodivergent lens comes in, and it's why there's no separate chapter on it. The CIPD's 2024 report on neuroinclusion recommends inviting adjustment requests from everyone, so asking becomes normal, and not assuming the usual ways of communicating feel accessible to everyone. It argues that a lot of what helps neurodivergent people helps the whole workforce (Thompson and Miller, 2024). It was co-written by the CIPD and the head of a company that sells neuroinclusion training, and those are recommendations drawn from UK surveys, not findings the surveys tested. In practice that means writing down what most companies leave implicit: who to ask about what, what "urgent" means here, how long a reply should take, and what good work looks like. Nobody loses from that being written down.

Why it isn't three months of documents#

It's tempting to solve onboarding by writing everything down and handing it over. Bauer's guide is blunt about this: reading handbooks and websites reveals very little about culture and unwritten rules, and writing a formal plan on its own doesn't help anyone succeed (Bauer, 2010).

The research on how newcomers actually learn backs that up. In a study of 205 new accountants, people mostly asked directly when they needed technical information, but picked up the rest (what's expected of them, how they're doing, what's acceptable) mainly by watching the people around them. Seeking information was linked to being more satisfied, performing better and being less likely to want to leave (Morrison, 1993). Documents are reference material. People are the onboarding.

So the written material in this playbook does a narrower job. It answers the questions someone shouldn't have to ask out loud, and it points them to the person for everything else. That's also why I'd rather a new hire read five things that matter than fifty that might.

Where Volume 1 comes in#

If you've built levels using Volume 1, onboarding is the first time a new hire sees what their level actually means. A level description says what's expected in general terms. Onboarding turns that into this role, this team, these first six months.

My suggestion is simple. In the first week, sit down with the new hire and their level description, and agree what each part looks like in practice for them by the end of month three. Write that down. It becomes the yardstick for every check-in after, and it means nobody gets judged against expectations they never saw.

An example#

This is illustrative, not a real company.

Remember the manager from section 1? Six reports, a seventh starting Monday, and a 40-page wiki.

Instead of sending the wiki, they pick out the five pages that answer things a new person would feel awkward asking: how to book time off, what the team means by "urgent", who signs off what, where the real decisions get made, and the level description for the role. Everything else stays in the wiki as reference. They book a buddy and a 30-minute slot with each of three colleagues the new hire will work with most.

It takes them about an hour. Most of the onboarding now happens in conversations they've set up, not in a reading list.

How sure is this?#

The link between these five needs and how new hires get on is one of the stronger findings in this area, with large meta-analyses behind it. Most of that research is correlational, though. It shows which things go together, not proof that changing one causes the other. The evidence that onboarding programmes as a whole help people stay is stronger, because it comes from field experiments (section 2). The case for explicitness rests on that research plus the CIPD report, and on my own experience, which I've told you about.

What would change my mind: good evidence that new hires who are mainly given documents to work through settle in, and feel accepted, just as well as those whose onboarding is built around people.

4. The five pillars#

Section last reviewed 9 November 2026.

Who it's for: People and ops leads designing onboarding, and line managers running it.

Everything a new hire needs to learn falls into five areas: the business, how operations fit together, their role, the people and culture, and where they could grow. Running underneath all five is one thing they need from day one: knowing where to take a problem when something goes wrong.

The pillars are my way of organising this. They line up closely with the four building blocks in Bauer's guide for the SHRM Foundation (compliance, clarification, culture and connection), with compliance left to local law and growth added (Bauer, 2010).

Each pillar holds up the same goal, and none of them stands without knowing where to take a problem.

The pillars at a glance#

Pillar What they need to know Who owns it They've got it when they can…
Business What we do, for whom, who leads, why teams sit where they do Founder or a senior leader Explain in two minutes how their work connects to what the customer pays for
Operations How teams and processes hand work to each other, and why Manager, with a peer from a neighbouring team Say where their work comes from and where it goes next, without checking
The role Daily work, expectations, what good looks like Manager Describe what good looks like in their role in their own words, and their manager agrees
People and culture How we communicate, how conflict and unfairness are handled, the culture as it's actually lived Manager and buddy Predict how their team would react to a mistake, a disagreement or an urgent request
Growth How levels and paths work, and what they want from work right now Manager, with People support Say what they want from the next year, and know the manager has heard it
Underneath all five: where to take a problem Who to go to for what, and what to do if that person is the problem People lead, with every manager Name the right route for three different things going wrong

That last column is the test. It's not "they attended the session". It's "they can do the thing". Section 9 builds on these for measuring whether onboarding is working.

Business#

This is the big picture: what the company does, who it serves, who leads and why the structure looks the way it does. It sounds like the easiest pillar, and it's the one most often skipped, because people assume it's obvious. It isn't obvious from the inside of week one.

Keep it short and keep it human. A 30-minute conversation with a founder or senior leader beats a slide deck, and it tells the new hire they're worth someone's time. Bauer's guide includes understanding goals, politics and the company's own language as part of knowing the culture, and this pillar is where that starts.

Operations#

This is how the parts connect: who hands what to whom, which processes exist and why, and which systems hold what. It's where experienced hires trip up most, because they bring a working model from their last job and it's almost right.

The research on star analysts in section 2 is the reason this pillar exists. A lot of what made people good at their last job lived in the systems and relationships around them. The quickest fix I know is a short conversation with someone in the team before and after them in the chain. "What do you need from me, and what do I need from you?" covers most of it.

The role#

This is what they do all day, what's expected and what good looks like. It has strong research behind it. Knowing what's expected is one of the most consistent predictors of how well new hires settle (section 3).

Write it down. A role description from the job ad isn't enough, because it was written to attract people, not to guide them. Use the level description from Volume 1 if you have one, and agree in week one what each part means for this person by month three. Then give them real work early, with feedback, because confidence comes from doing, not reading.

People and culture#

This is the widest pillar, so here's what it needs to cover.

  • How we communicate. Which channel for what, how fast people are expected to reply, when to call rather than message, and how all of that works in person, remote and hybrid. Remote new hires find the social side hardest. A survey of 267 Microsoft developers who started remotely during the pandemic found connection with the team was the main struggle, and recommended cameras on, scheduled one-to-ones and buddies (Rodeghero et al., 2021). That's one company and one industry, so treat it as a strong hint, not a rule. Cameras on is the study's recommendation. If your team adopts it, agree norms that let people turn theirs off without having to explain why.
  • How conflict is handled. What happens when two people disagree, and what's expected of each of them. Section 7 goes into this properly.
  • How perceived unfairness is handled. Where someone takes it when they think a decision wasn't fair, and what they'll hear back. Also section 7.
  • Self-awareness. Space for the new hire to say how they work best, and a way for them to find out how they're coming across. There's research behind starting with the person rather than the company. In the call centre experiment in section 2, new hires asked about their own strengths on day one were less likely to quit early (Cable, Gino and Staats, 2013). A short "how I work" note from the new hire, and one from their manager, is my favourite way to start.
  • The culture we claim and the one we live. Most companies have values on a wall. What new hires actually have to learn is how their team behaves, and teams within the same company can differ a lot. Write down both: the company's stated norms, and the team's actual ones. If they don't match, that's useful to know, for you as much as for the new hire.

Growth#

This is where the role, and the person, could go from here. People join at different points in their lives and want different things from work. Some want more money or more responsibility. Some want to learn a different skill. Some want more time in the office, or less. Some want to keep getting better at exactly what they're doing now. None of those is the wrong answer.

Ask early. In the first month, have a "what do you want from work right now?" conversation, and have it again around month six, because answers change once people know the place. Treat what you hear as information, not a promise either of you has to keep. Gallup's survey work found people who strongly agreed they had a clear plan for their development were 3.5 times as likely to strongly agree their onboarding had been exceptional (Gallup, 2019). That's a survey link, not proof one causes the other, and Gallup sells consulting on this.

If what someone wants means doing things differently for them, that's worth doing with care. Research on individually agreed arrangements links them to benefits for the person (Liao, Wayne and Rousseau, 2016), and critics argue they can strain relationships with colleagues who don't get the same (Conway and Coyle-Shapiro, 2016). Section 7 covers how to be fair about it without sharing what's private.

Underneath all five: where to take a problem#

Every pillar eventually meets something going wrong. A missed handover, a disagreement, a decision that feels unfair, a manager who's the problem. New hires need to know the route before they need it, because the moment something goes wrong is the worst moment to start working out who to ask.

Keep it to one page: for each kind of problem, who to go to first, and who to go to if that person is the problem or doesn't respond. Section 10 has a template. Walk through it in week one, and come back to it once something has actually happened, so they see it working.

Not drowning them#

Five pillars sounds like a lot. It isn't, if you pace it. Each pillar has a handful of things they need in the first week, more by month three, and the rest by month six. Section 5 lays out when. My rule of thumb is that if something won't matter to them for a month, it doesn't belong in week one.

An example#

This is illustrative, not a real company.

Remember the founder from section 1? Twelve people, first hire who isn't a friend, laptop ordered.

She writes one page per pillar, mostly bullet points, in an afternoon. The business page is what she'd say over coffee. The operations page names the two people the new hire will hand work to and from. The role page is three things good looks like by month three. The culture page includes something she's never written down before: that the team says yes to things in meetings and pushes back in messages afterwards, and that's fine. The escalation page is short, because at twelve people the answer is often her. So it also names an outside adviser for anything that's about her.

How sure is this?#

The five pillars are my framework. They map onto well-established research on what new hires need (section 3) and onto Bauer's building blocks, but nobody has tested this exact split. Some of the content inside them, like the role pillar, has strong evidence. Some, like writing down the culture as it's lived, is my view.

What would change my mind: evidence that organising onboarding by pillar, rather than by week or by topic, makes no difference to how quickly people settle. If that turned up, I'd keep the content and drop the structure.

5. The six-month arc#

Section last reviewed 9 November 2026.

Who it's for: People and ops leads planning the shape, line managers running it, and new hires who want to know what's coming.

Onboarding runs from the day the offer is accepted to about six months in. The organisation does most of its work at the start, before and just after day one. The new hire's learning is spread across the whole six months. Roughly, the first three months are for learning the job, and the next three are for learning how the organisation actually works together.

The day counts in this section are examples, not rules. There's no research comparing a 30-day check-in with a 45-day one, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. What the research does show is that timing matters in a broader way. Support from managers and colleagues tends to drop off within the first 90 days, and the support people get early on matters more for how things turn out than later changes do (Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2013). So put the effort in early, and plan how it carries on, rather than letting it fade on its own.

Offer accepted to day one#

This is the organisation's busiest stage and the new hire's lightest. They aren't being paid yet. They may have just come out of a negotiation, and they're probably working their notice somewhere else. Asking them to read a handbook or complete training now is unpaid work, and it piles onto a first day that's already stressful. So don't.

What they do need is one short message, no more than a page, that tells them:

  • who'll be in touch before they start, and when
  • their manager's name
  • their buddy's name, and how long the buddy arrangement runs
  • when they'll meet the team
  • when their laptop arrives, and when their accounts and access go live
  • what day one looks like, roughly by blocks

That's it. Anything else you send is clearly marked optional, and skipping it costs them nothing on day one.

If there's something hard about the role that they should know, this is a good place to start. A meta-analysis of 52 studies found that realistic previews of a job reduce people leaving mainly because they signal that the organisation is honest, and that the most effective ones may be spoken or written and given after someone's been hired (Earnest, Allen and Landis, 2011; publisher). The research doesn't say previews should be short. If anything, previews lasting more than an hour did slightly better (QIC-WD summary). Keeping this message short is my choice, for the reasons above. What matters is that it's honest.

Meanwhile, the organisation is busy. Equipment and accounts get set up and tested. The buddy gets briefed. The manager drafts a 30-day plan and gets a short reminder of what to do on day one (section 10 has one). The team hears who's joining and when.

Week one#

The aim is that by Friday they feel welcome, know where things are, and have done one small piece of real work.

Spend the time on people, not paperwork. The manager meets them on day one, ideally first thing. Go through their level description together and agree what it looks like for them by month three. Walk through the escalation map from section 4. Swap "how I work" notes. Give them one small, real task they can finish this week. Real work builds confidence faster than reading does.

There's evidence this kind of start matters. In the call centre experiment from section 2, the change that made new hires less likely to quit was an hour on day one spent on who they were and what they were good at (Cable, Gino and Staats, 2013).

Weeks two to four#

Now the work gets real. Weekly one-to-ones with the manager, short is fine. A 30-day plan agreed together. Introductions to the people they'll hand work to and from (the operations pillar). Regular time with the buddy.

On the buddy, Microsoft's published figures are worth knowing. The more often new hires met their buddy in the first 90 days, the more of them said it helped them get productive quickly, rising from 56% of those who met at least once to 97% of those who met more than eight times (Microsoft Workplace Insights, 2019). That's what people said about themselves, at one company, and people who meet their buddy more might be better connected anyway. But it fits with everything else here: regular contact early beats one welcome coffee.

This is also when to have the first "what do you want from work right now?" conversation from the growth pillar.

Months two and three#

This is where they get confident in the job. Give them something to own. Keep one-to-ones going, even when things seem fine, because this is exactly when support tends to drop away. Around 90 days, check the five needs from section 3 together: do they know what's expected, believe they can do it, feel accepted, understand how things work, and feel they fit?

If you chose a 90-day buddy arrangement, it ends here. Decide in advance what replaces it: usually a wider circle of colleagues, and the manager keeping an eye out for anyone who's gone quiet.

Months four to six#

Ninety days is enough to learn the job. It isn't enough to learn how the organisation really works together: who decides what, how other teams think, where the unwritten politics sit. That takes longer, and in my experience it's the stage onboarding most often forgets.

Widen their network across teams on purpose. Have the growth conversation again, because the answers often change once people know the place. Then close with a six-month review that runs both ways: how they're doing, and how the onboarding did. Ask the new hire and the manager separately whether onboarding feels finished. An unpublished conference poster found hourly workers felt fully on board after about three and a half weeks, while their supervisors put it at about three months (Krauss, 2010, cited in Bauer, 2010). Gaps like that are worth finding before they turn into a probation surprise.

Where probation fits#

If you have a probation period, it runs alongside this arc, not instead of it. The expectations a new hire is judged against should be the ones written down in week one and checked at 90 days. Section 8 goes into how to do this without misreading people.

An example#

This is illustrative, not a real company.

Remember the new hire from section 1? Three weeks in, keeping quiet, not sure what they're allowed to ask, and hearing the word probation.

In a company running this arc, week three looks different. They got a one-page message before they started, so they knew their buddy's name and what day one would hold. Their manager has a weekly one-to-one with them, and ends each one with the same question: "What's one thing you've wondered about but haven't asked?" Their buddy has met them four times. Probation has come up, but as the list of things agreed in week one, which they can already see they're working through.

They're still a bit quiet. But now that's someone settling in, not someone drifting.

How sure is this?#

The shape here, effort early and support kept up deliberately, rests on solid research. The specific days are convention. Bauer's guide suggests checking in at 30, 60, 90 and 120 days and up to a year, and Google's former head of people describes monthly check-ins for six months. Both are practice, not tested timings. Use them as starting points and adjust to the work.

What would change my mind: good evidence that a particular check-in schedule, or a particular buddy length, leads to better outcomes than others. If someone has run that comparison, I'd change these examples to match.

6. Who does what#

Section last reviewed 9 November 2026.

Who it's for: line managers first, then People and ops leads, and founders doing all of it themselves.

The manager matters most. The buddy covers what the manager can't. The team makes someone feel they belong. People and ops keep the system running so none of that depends on memory or goodwill. In a small company, the founder may be most of these at once.

The manager#

If you only fix one role, fix this one. Section 2 covered the study where what managers did predicted how new hires settled, and the new hire's own question-asking didn't (Bauer and Green, 1998). Gallup's survey work points the same way: people whose manager took an active part in their onboarding were 3.4 times as likely to strongly agree it had been exceptional (Gallup, 2019). That's a survey, and Gallup sells consulting on this, but it fits the research.

There's a trap worth knowing about. A study of new software engineers and their managers found that managers gave more information and support to the newcomers they saw as proactive, and that support helped those newcomers settle (Ellis et al., 2017; publisher). So a quiet new hire can end up with less help, exactly when they need more. The fix is simple: put the one-to-ones in the diary and keep them, rather than waiting to be asked.

Here's what I'd expect it to take, for a manager with around six reports and one new starter. These are my estimates, not research, and they'll vary with the role.

Stage Manager time What it's spent on
Before day one About 1 hour The "what happens next" message, briefing the buddy, drafting a 30-day plan
Week one About 4 hours Day-one welcome, the level description conversation, escalation map, "how I work" notes, setting the first task
Weeks two to four About 1.5 hours a week Weekly one-to-one, introductions, feedback on early work
Months two and three About 1 hour a week One-to-ones, the 90-day check on the five needs
Months four to six About 45 minutes a week One-to-ones, introductions across teams, the six-month review

That's real time. Set it against the manager hours on your own cost table in section 2. And most of it is time you'd spend anyway, just spent earlier and on purpose.

Keeping it from becoming box-ticking#

A checklist is useful as a reminder. It goes wrong when finishing the checklist becomes the goal. A manager can tick "30-day plan agreed" without the new hire understanding a word of it.

So use the checklist to remember what to do, and use the "they've got it when they can…" column from section 4 to see whether it worked. If you only have time for one, have the conversation and skip the form.

The buddy#

A buddy is a colleague, usually a peer, who helps the new hire with the things they'd rather not ask their manager. Research on how newcomers learn suggests why that matters: much of what they pick up about what's expected and what's acceptable comes from watching the people around them rather than asking (Morrison, 1993). A buddy gives them someone to watch and someone safe to ask. And a study of short-term peer mentors, assigned to new students, found the support they gave was linked to newcomers settling in and coping with stress (Allen, McManus and Russell, 1999). That sample was students, not employees, so it's a close cousin of a work buddy rather than the same thing.

There's no single right way to run a buddy arrangement, and no research on how long one should last. These are the choices you'll make, with what evidence there is for each.

Choice Options What the evidence says
How long 90 days, six months, or tied to a milestone in the work Nothing on length. Microsoft's figures suggest regular contact in the first 90 days helps (section 5)
Same team or another team Same team for the work, another team for a wider view No direct evidence. My default is the same team, so the buddy knows the work
What level A peer, not the manager and not the manager's boss The peer research above. A senior buddy can make honest questions harder
How often Weekly to start, then as needed Microsoft's figures again: more meetings went with more new hires saying it helped
What's in and out In: how things really work, who to ask, what's normal. Out: judging performance, being a second manager My view. A buddy who reports back on performance is a buddy nobody confides in
How they're matched Both people get a say In formal mentoring schemes, having input into the pairing went with better relationships (Allen, Eby and Lentz, 2006; publisher)
Voluntary or not Volunteers, with time protected The same researchers note formal mentoring tends to do less than relationships people choose. My view is that a reluctant buddy is worse than none
Briefing A one-page buddy brief (section 10) Good-quality preparation for both people was linked to better relationships in the same study

Whatever length you pick, tell the new hire before they start, and decide upfront what happens when it ends.

The team#

Feeling accepted by colleagues is one of the five needs from section 3, and the latest research review singled out how much it matters (Bauer et al., 2025). Support from colleagues also tends to drop away in the first few months (Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2013).

The team's job is small but it matters. Know who's joining and when. Name two or three people who'll make time for a proper conversation in the first month. Include them in the ordinary stuff: lunch, the team chat, the in-jokes once they're explained. Nobody should be left out in week two because everyone assumed someone else had invited them.

People and ops#

People and ops don't run each onboarding. They run the system that makes every onboarding work, whoever the manager is:

  • the templates in section 10, kept up to date
  • the pre-start logistics, so equipment and access are ready on day one
  • the escalation map, and making sure every manager knows it
  • the measures in section 9, and spotting when a team's onboarding is slipping
  • catching it when a manager is on leave or overloaded, so the new hire isn't left alone

That last one is where I've most often seen onboarding quietly fail. Someone needs to be watching for it.

The founder, when there's no one else#

If you're making your first hires, you're the manager, the People team and quite possibly the buddy too. That's fine. Do the manager's part properly and keep the rest light. Two things I'd hold on to: give the new hire someone other than you to ask the awkward questions, even an outside adviser or a friendly peer at another company, and name that person on the escalation map for anything that's about you.

Most research on onboarding comes from large organisations. I haven't found much on very small companies making their first hires, so this part is my view.

An example#

This is illustrative, not a real company.

Remember the manager from section 1? Six reports, a seventh starting Monday, no free afternoon until the end of the month.

They look at the time table and block it out: an hour this week, four hours in the new starter's first week, then a weekly one-to-one plus time for introductions and feedback. They ask the team who'd like to be the buddy, and two people volunteer. They let the new starter choose after a quick coffee with each. They tell the People lead they're on leave in week five, and she books in a check-in with the new hire for that week.

It's not much. It's mostly a diary and two conversations. But the kind of gaps that sank the senior hires in section 2's example are closed before they open.

How sure is this?#

The manager's central role is one of the stronger findings in this area. The buddy evidence is borrowed from research on peer mentoring and one company's published figures, so it's suggestive rather than settled. The time estimates and the founder advice are my view.

What would change my mind: evidence that buddies make no difference once the manager is doing their part well. If that turned up, I'd make the buddy optional for small teams rather than a default.

7. Handling tensions before they happen#

Section last reviewed 9 November 2026.

Who it's for: line managers, People and ops leads, and new hires.

Every team runs into disagreements and moments where something feels unfair. The worst time to learn how your organisation deals with them is in the middle of one. So teach it during onboarding, before anything has gone wrong, while it's still a calm conversation about how things work here.

Why fairness needs explaining upfront#

Research on fairness at work splits it into four kinds, and they turn out to be separate things. A meta-analysis of 183 studies found each one was linked to satisfaction, commitment, trust in managers, people pulling back from their work, and performance (Colquitt et al., 2001; publisher).

Kind of fairness The question people ask What onboarding can do about it
Outcomes "Did I get what I deserved, compared with others?" Not much directly. But the other three shape how outcomes are received
Process "Was the decision made in a fair way, and could I have a say?" Explain how decisions get made here, and how someone can raise a concern
Respect "Was I treated with dignity?" Set the standard for how disagreements are handled, in writing
Explanation "Was I told why, honestly and in time?" Make it normal to ask why, and normal for managers to answer

The useful bit for onboarding is this. Even when people can't be told the reasons behind a decision, they can know how decisions like it are made and where to take a concern. That's often enough for something to feel fair. Both scenarios below depend on it.

Speaking up and hierarchy#

How comfortable people are disagreeing with a manager, challenging a decision or delivering bad news varies a lot between companies, and between teams in the same company. New hires bring the norms of their last job with them, and nobody tells them which ones still apply.

So write your team's norms down. Not the company values, but the actual answers to questions like these:

  • Is it fine to disagree with your manager in a meeting, or do you raise it afterwards?
  • How do you challenge a decision that's already been made?
  • What happens when someone brings bad news early?
  • Who usually speaks first in meetings, and is that a habit or a rule?
  • If you're stuck, how long should you try before asking?

These norms belong to your organisation and your team. Don't put them down to anyone's nationality, ethnicity or background, and don't let anyone else do that either. What you're describing is how this team works, which is something people can learn.

There's research on why this matters for learning. A study of 51 teams in a manufacturing company found that where people felt safe to take interpersonal risks, they did more of the things teams learn from, and that learning went with better performance (Edmondson, 1999; publisher). That's one company, and teams rather than newcomers. A four-wave study of 198 newcomers also found that speaking up and staying silent developed along separate paths, so a new hire going quiet isn't simply the opposite of one speaking up (Reissner, Guenter and de Jong, 2024).

Section 10 has a template for writing down your team's speaking-up norms.

Scenario 1: "Why does she get to work from home?"#

This is illustrative, not a real company.

Ola works fully remotely. The reason is a health condition she has shared with her manager, Sam, and with HR, and with nobody else. Two newer colleagues, Dev and Marta, are in the office four days a week. They've started asking Sam, half joking, why Ola gets "special treatment".

Sam can't explain, and shouldn't hint. No "it's complicated", no meaningful look, no "it's a medical thing". Any of those tells people something about Ola that isn't theirs to know.

What Sam can do is make the process visible:

  1. Say what's true without saying why. "Working arrangements here are agreed one person at a time, for reasons that stay between that person, me and HR. That's the same for everyone, including you."
  2. Show them the route. "If you'd like a different arrangement, here's how to ask, and here's what happens next." Then make sure that route actually exists and is written down.
  3. Take the underlying question seriously. Sometimes "why does she get that?" really means "I'd like that too" or "I don't feel trusted". Ask.
  4. Draw the line on speculation, kindly. "I'm not going to talk about anyone else's arrangement, and I'd ask you not to either."

Research on individually agreed arrangements finds they can help the person and also strain things with colleagues who don't get the same (Liao, Wayne and Rousseau, 2016; Conway and Coyle-Shapiro, 2016). Making the process open is how you keep the benefit without the resentment.

This is also why it belongs in onboarding. If Dev and Marta had been told in their first month how working arrangements are agreed here, the question would mostly answer itself. Rules on health information and workplace adjustments vary by country, so check local law.

Scenario 2: "Why is it always him on stage?"#

This is illustrative, not a real company.

Tomás has been picked to speak at three industry conferences this year. Aisling, who joined around the same time and does similar work, hasn't been asked once. She's started to resent it, and to resent Tomás, who has no idea.

How Aisling can raise it. Go to the manager, Ruth, not to Tomás. Make it about the process, not the person, and ask for what she wants:

"I've noticed Tomás has spoken at the last three conferences. Can you tell me how speakers get chosen? I'd like to be considered, and I'd like to know what I'd need to do."

That's specific, it doesn't accuse anyone, and it leaves Ruth room to answer well.

How Ruth should respond.

  1. Thank her for raising it directly. That's exactly the behaviour you want.
  2. Be honest about how choices were made. If there was a process, explain it. If it was "Tomás asked first" or "I thought of him", say so. That's the honest answer more often than people want to admit.
  3. Fix the process, not just this case. Agree how speakers will be chosen from now on: who decides, on what basis, and how people put themselves forward. Write it down and tell the whole team, not just Aisling.
  4. Give Aisling a concrete path. The next suitable event, any support she needs, and a date to check back.
  5. Keep Tomás out of it. He didn't make the decisions. He shouldn't carry them.

In a different setting, pay decisions, one study found that adding accountability and openness to how decisions were made reduced gaps between groups (Castilla, 2015). Speaking slots aren't pay, but the principle carries: visible criteria make it harder for opportunities to drift to the same people.

Again, this is easier to prevent than fix. If the team's onboarding had included "here's how opportunities like speaking, projects and travel get handed out", Aisling wouldn't have had to work out whether she was allowed to ask.

Teaching it before it's needed#

My suggestion: some time in weeks two to four, the manager walks the new hire through how fairness concerns and disagreements are handled here, using the team's written norms and a scenario or two like the ones above. It takes half an hour. Then, the first time something real happens, the new hire already knows the route, and so does everyone else.

An example#

This is illustrative, not a real company.

Remember the People person from section 1? After the two senior hires left, she found that one of them had been quietly sitting on a disagreement with a decision for weeks. They didn't know whether challenging it was allowed.

She asks each team lead to spend twenty minutes answering the five speaking-up questions with their team. The answers turn out to be different across teams, which is exactly the point. Each team's answers go into its onboarding pack. A new starter in one team now learns, in week two, that disagreeing in a meeting is welcome there. A new starter in another learns that the team prefers a message first and a conversation after. Neither has to guess.

How sure is this?#

The research on fairness at work is strong and has been tested in many settings, though not specifically on newcomers. The value of explaining process when you can't explain reasons follows from it. The scenarios and the idea of teaching all this upfront are my view. I haven't found a study that tested teaching conflict and fairness routes during onboarding.

What would change my mind: evidence that explaining these routes early makes no difference to how fair people find later decisions, or makes people more likely to complain about small things. I'd want to see it before dropping the advice, but I'd take it seriously.

8. Probation without misreading people#

Section last reviewed 9 November 2026.

Who it's for: line managers making probation calls, and People and ops leads who back them up.

Only judge someone against expectations they were actually given. Don't mistake quiet for checked out. And before you decide someone was the wrong hire, rule out the possibility that onboarding let them down. Once you've done that, if it really is a mismatch, end it quickly and decently. That's kinder to everyone than letting it drag on.

Probation rules vary a lot by country and contract, so check local law before you change how yours works.

Quiet isn't the same as disengaged#

Someone who was lively at interview can go quiet in their first few weeks. That's often a good sign. At interview they were performing. Now they're taking everything in, working out who's who and what's safe to say. Being reserved while you learn a place isn't the same as not caring about it.

The research gives three reasons to be careful here:

  • Quiet and speaking up are separate things. A study following 198 newcomers found that speaking up and staying silent developed along different paths over time (Reissner, Guenter and de Jong, 2024). Someone saying less in meetings tells you less than you'd think.
  • Asking questions partly depends on personality. New hires who were more extraverted and open tended to seek feedback and build relationships more (Wanberg and Kammeyer-Mueller, 2000; publisher). A quieter person isn't necessarily less committed, just less likely to push.
  • Your read can become the outcome. Managers tend to give more support to newcomers they see as proactive (Ellis et al., 2017). So if you decide early that someone's disengaged, you may give them less, and they really will struggle.

So don't judge by volume. Look at whether their questions are getting more specific over time, even if there aren't many of them. Look at whether they follow through on what they've agreed to do, and whether they're asking somebody, even if it's the buddy rather than you. If you're unsure, ask them directly how it's going and what would help. Don't guess at reasons, and don't diagnose.

Make expectations explicit before you judge anyone against them#

This is the most important part of the section. Probation only works if the person knows what they're being measured against, from the start.

In week one, write down three to five things that good looks like by the end of probation. Tie them to the level description where you have one (section 3). Make them about what the person can do, not how they come across. Share them, agree them, and keep a copy each. Then check them together around 30, 60 and 90 days, or whatever points fit your probation length.

Two rules make this fair:

  1. No surprise criteria. If something new starts to matter, say so as soon as you notice, and give them time to show it. Don't add it at the final review.
  2. Feedback before the verdict. If something's off track at day 30, say so at day 30. Nobody should hear about a problem for the first time at the end of probation.

This is the fairness research from section 7 put to use. People accept hard outcomes better when they can see the process was fair and the reasons were explained (Colquitt et al., 2001). Section 10 has a probation expectations template.

Mismatch or onboarding?#

Section 2 gave four quick questions. Here's the fuller version. Work through it before any decision that someone isn't working out. If the answer to a question is no, that's an onboarding gap. Fix it, give it time, and then look again.

Pillar The question If the answer is no
Business Can they explain how their work connects to what customers pay for? They were never given the big picture
Operations Do they know where their work comes from and where it goes next? They were never introduced to the chain around them
The role Were the expectations written down and agreed in week one, and have they had feedback against them since? They're being judged on something they couldn't see
People and culture Do they know how this team communicates, disagrees and asks for help? They're following the norms of their last job
Growth Has anyone asked what they want from this role? You may be reading a mismatch in what they want as a lack of effort
Where to take a problem Do they know who to go to when something goes wrong? Problems are sitting with them instead of moving
The manager Have one-to-ones happened as planned, including through any leave or busy patches? The support was never there

If every answer is yes and they're still struggling after a fair chance, you may be looking at a genuine mismatch.

When it really is a mismatch#

Some hires genuinely don't work out. The role turned out to be different from what either side expected. The gap in skills is too big to close in a reasonable time, even with support. Or the way they want to work clashes with how the team works, even after everything was made explicit. None of those are anyone's failure as a person.

When it's real, here's how I'd handle it:

  • Name it early and privately. As soon as you think it might not work, say so, with specifics, and say what would need to change.
  • Give a real chance where it's reasonable. A short, clear period with agreed goals and support. Not a formality on the way to a decision you've already made.
  • If it's ending, end it quickly and with dignity. Long, drawn-out exits are worse for the person and the team. Be honest about the reasons, agree what gets said to colleagues, and help with the move where you can.
  • Learn from it. Was the mismatch visible at hiring? Was something missing in onboarding after all? Write down one thing you'll change.

Keeping fast exits possible matters. It stops a bad fit turning into months of misery for everyone. It just has to come after onboarding has been ruled out, not instead of it.

An example#

This is illustrative, not a real company.

Remember the new hire from section 1? Three weeks in, quiet, not sure what they're allowed to ask, and their manager has started mentioning probation.

Before saying anything more about probation, the manager goes through the table. Business, operations and the role are fine: the expectations were written down in week one, and the work is on track. People and culture is the gap. The new hire's last company frowned on questions in shared channels, so they've been holding them all until their one-to-one. Nobody had told them that here, asking in the team channel is normal and welcome.

The manager says so, and asks the buddy to answer the first few in the channel to show it's fine. By week eight the new hire is asking a couple of specific questions a day. The probation conversation at 90 days is about the three agreed outcomes, all on track, and nothing else.

How sure is this?#

The research behind "don't judge by volume" is reasonably solid, especially the finding that managers support people they see as proactive more. The idea of agreeing expectations upfront rests on the fairness research. The diagnostic table and the advice on handling mismatches are my view. I haven't found any study measuring how many probation failures were really onboarding failures.

What would change my mind: evidence that people who go quiet early really do go on to perform worse, even after you account for how they were onboarded and supported. If that turned up, I'd keep the advice about fair process, but I'd change what I say about reading quiet.

9. Is it working, and adjusting it#

Section last reviewed 9 November 2026.

Who it's for: everyone, with a part each for founders, People and ops leads, line managers and new hires.

You'll know onboarding is working when new hires can do the things in section 4's "they've got it when they can…" column, when their questions get steadily more specific, and when fewer of them leave early. Watch the early signals so you can fix things while they still matter. Use the later results to check whether the fixes worked.

Early signals and later results#

Two kinds of measure are worth keeping apart.

  • Early signals show up in weeks. They tell you something's going wrong while you can still do something about it. How many questions someone's asking, and what kind, is one.
  • Later results show up in months or a year. They tell you whether onboarding worked overall. How many people leave in their first six months is one.

If you only track later results, you find out about problems after the people have gone. If you only track early signals, you never find out whether any of it made a difference. You want a few of each.

Questions: the signal everyone can see#

A new hire asking no questions at all is a warning sign, not a good one. It usually means one of three things:

  1. What they've been given is too vague to know what to ask about.
  2. The way it's delivered discourages asking. Questions get a sigh, a "didn't you read the wiki?", or no reply.
  3. They're not engaged. This is possible, but it's the last one to check, not the first.

Check the first two before you reach the third. Most of the time the fix is on your side.

What healthy looks like is a steady flow of questions that get more specific over time. In week one: "Where do I find the customer list?" By month two: "Should I flag this account to sales before or after I've spoken to them?" The second question shows they understand how things connect. Volume matters less than direction, especially for quieter people (section 8).

What to measure, by who's asking#

These are my suggestions. Pick a few that answer questions you actually have, rather than tracking everything.

Founders and executives

New hires

A self-check you can run any week:

  • Do I know what good looks like in my role, and is it written down?
  • Do I know who to ask about what, and does asking feel fine?
  • Do I know where to take a problem, including if my manager is the problem?
  • Do I understand where my work comes from and where it goes?
  • Has anyone asked me what I want from this job?

If any answer is no, that's worth raising. It's a gap in onboarding, not in you.

A short check on the five needs#

Here's a five-question check you can send at 30, 90 and 180 days. Ask people to answer each from 1 (not at all) to 5 (completely), with a box for comments.

  1. I know what's expected of me in my role.
  2. I feel confident I can do my job well.
  3. I feel accepted by the people I work with.
  4. I understand how things really work here.
  5. I feel this is a good fit for me.

This is my own wording, built on the five needs in section 3. It isn't a tested scale. Researchers have built validated measures of newcomer adjustment, and I haven't yet checked them closely enough to recommend one. If you want something more rigorous, that's where to look. For most teams, the comments box will tell you more than the numbers will.

Adjusting it#

When something's off, use the signals to work out which part to fix. Fix one thing at a time, so you can tell what made the difference.

What you're seeing Most likely gap First thing to try
Lots of questions about where things are and who does what, still in month two Operations Introduce them to the people before and after them in the chain
Busy, but on the wrong things The role Rewrite the expectations together, more concretely
Quiet in groups, fine in one-to-ones People and culture Have the buddy model asking in the open, and write down the team's speaking-up norms
Problems surface late, or sideways Where to take a problem Walk through the escalation map again, using a real example
Good work but restless by month four Growth Have the "what do you want from work?" conversation again
One team's new hires struggle and others don't That team's process or manager Look at that team's one-to-ones and milestones before anything else

As you grow. At your first hire, all of this is one founder and a few pages. Somewhere around ten people, you'll want a named buddy and written expectations as standard. Somewhere past fifty, someone needs to own the system, keep the templates current and look at the numbers each quarter. Those thresholds are my rough guide, not research.

Remote and hybrid. Remote new hires in one pandemic-era study found the social side hardest (Rodeghero et al., 2021). My guess is that the "feel accepted" score will lag for remote starters, and build in more scheduled contact rather than less.

An example#

This is illustrative, not a real company.

Remember the People person from section 1? After the two senior exits, she sets up three things: the five-question check at 30, 90 and 180 days, the "is onboarding finished?" question to each new hire and their manager, and a quarterly look at early leavers by team.

The first round shows a pattern. Most new hires score well on knowing what's expected, but one team's scores on understanding how things work stay low at 90 days. The comments say the same thing in different words: nobody explained how that team's work connects to the two teams it depends on. She sets up the operations introductions for that team, and the next round's scores come up. It took one quarter to spot and one conversation to fix.

How sure is this?#

Early leavers and time to productivity are simple to track, and the five needs come from solid research. But the specific measures, the five-question check and the thresholds are my design. None of them has been tested as a set, and I haven't found a published benchmark for what "good" looks like.

What would change my mind: a validated short measure of newcomer adjustment that's free to use and quick to run. If I find one that fits, I'll swap it in for my five questions.

Tomorrow [name] starts. Here are the five things that make the biggest difference:

11. For the new hire#

Section last reviewed 9 November 2026.

Who it's for: you, if you've just started somewhere or you're about to. Managers may find it useful to read from the other side.

If you only read one page of this playbook, make it this one. Your first six months are for learning. Making that easy is the organisation's job, not yours. If parts of it are missing, that's a gap on their side, and you're allowed to point it out.

Before you start#

You shouldn't have to do anything. You haven't been paid yet, and you may still be working your notice somewhere else. What you should get is one short message: who'll be in touch, who your manager and buddy are, when you'll meet the team, when your laptop and access arrive, and roughly what day one looks like.

If you get a pile of reading instead, it can wait until you start. If you don't get the basics, ask for them. "Could you let me know who my manager is and what my first day looks like?" is a reasonable question, not a demanding one.

Why it takes six months#

However experienced you are, a new job comes with new people, new systems and a lot of unwritten rules. Research on hiring found that the experience people bring barely predicts how well they'll do in a new organisation. It gives you a head start, not a map (Van Iddekinge et al., 2019).

Roughly, the first three months are for learning the job. The next three are for learning how the place really works together: who decides what, how other teams think, what nobody writes down. Feeling slower than you'd like in month two is normal. It isn't a sign you made the wrong move.

Your questions are wanted#

The research on settling into a new job keeps coming back to the same few things: knowing what's expected of you, feeling confident you can do it, and feeling accepted by the people around you. Those are linked to people being more satisfied, doing better and staying (Bauer et al., 2007). Questions are how you get there.

A good sign is your questions getting more specific over time. "Where's the customer list?" in week one, and "Should I flag this to sales before I call them?" by month two. That shows you're learning how things connect.

A few things that make asking easier:

  • Use your buddy. That's what they're there for, especially for the questions you'd rather not ask your manager. At Microsoft, the more often new hires met their buddy in the first 90 days, the more of them said it helped them get productive quickly (Microsoft Workplace Insights, 2019).
  • Ask how people like to be asked. Some prefer a message, some a quick call, some a list once a day.
  • Say how urgent it is. "No rush, but…" or "I'm blocked on this" helps people help you.
  • Say what you've tried. "I've checked the wiki and asked Sam" saves everyone time.

If asking feels risky where you are, that tells you something about the environment, not about you.

What you can expect#

Good onboarding looks different everywhere, but you can reasonably expect most of this.

From your manager

  • Time with you on day one.
  • What good looks like in your role, written down and agreed in your first week or two.
  • Regular one-to-ones, kept even when things seem fine.
  • Feedback early, not saved up for a review.
  • A conversation about what you want from work, and another a few months later.
  • A clear answer to "where do I take a problem?", including if the problem is them.

From your buddy

  • How things really work here.
  • Someone safe to ask the small, awkward questions.
  • Not judging you, and not reporting on you.

From your team

  • Introductions to the people you'll work with most.
  • Being included in the ordinary stuff.

What's yours

  • Try real work early, even before you feel ready.
  • Ask, and keep asking.
  • Follow through on what you've agreed to do.
  • Say how you work best, and what you need.
  • Speak up when something's missing.

If you're quiet#

Going quiet in your first few weeks while you take everything in is fine. Saying less in meetings isn't the same as not caring, and research on newcomers found that speaking up and staying silent develop along separate paths (Reissner, Guenter and de Jong, 2024).

Not every manager knows that. So it helps to tell yours how you work: "I'm usually quiet in big meetings. I'll follow up in writing." And make sure you're asking someone, even if it's your buddy rather than your manager.

If you work better with things written down, agendas ahead of time, or instructions spelled out rather than hinted at, say so. Good onboarding does that for everyone anyway. You're not asking for a favour.

When something isn't right#

Run this check any week:

  • Do I know what good looks like in my role, and is it written down?
  • Do I know who to ask about what, and does asking feel fine?
  • Do I know where to take a problem, including if my manager is the problem?
  • Do I understand where my work comes from and where it goes?
  • Has anyone asked me what I want from this job?

If any answer is no, raise it with your manager or your buddy. It's a gap in onboarding, not in you. Something like: "I'm not sure what good looks like for me by month three. Could we write it down together?"

If you're on probation, you should know from your first week what you're being judged against, and hear early if something's off track. If you hear about a problem for the first time at the end, that's a failure of the process, and it's reasonable to ask how the criteria were set. Your rights during probation depend on your country and contract.

Sometimes a job genuinely isn't the right fit, even when everyone's done their part. That happens, and it isn't a failure on your part as a person. But make sure the gaps above have been filled before anyone decides that, including you.

An example#

This is illustrative, not a real company.

Remember the new hire from section 1? Three weeks in, keeping quiet, and their manager has started mentioning probation.

They run the check. Two answers are no: nothing about their role is written down, and they don't know where to take a problem. They ask their buddy how best to bring it up, and the buddy suggests the next one-to-one. There, they ask if they could agree in writing what good looks like by the end of probation. Their manager realises that step was skipped. They agree three outcomes and a check-in at 60 days. Probation stops being a vague worry and becomes a plan.

How sure is this?#

The evidence behind what new hires need, and how much the manager matters, is solid. The tips on asking, the scripts and what to expect are my view, built on that research and on what I've witnessed.

What would change my mind: hearing from new hires that raising gaps the way this page suggests backfired on them. If that's happened to you, I want to know, so I can make this page safer to follow.

How to read the sources#

  • Research is peer-reviewed or academic work. Where a finding is old, small, from one company or from a lab, the section that uses it says so.
  • Published frameworks are real frameworks organisations have put out for others to use. They show what's done, not what works.
  • Practitioner writing is experienced people and companies sharing what they've learned, including their own internal data and industry surveys. It's useful, but it's not research.
  • Professional bodies set standards for a profession. Worth reading, not neutral on everything.

Where a source's author or publisher sells the thing it recommends, the section that uses it says so.

Wherever there's a legal free copy, like the authors' own version or a university repository, that's the first link. The publisher's version, which may be behind a paywall, comes second as "publisher". Where there's no free copy I can find, the publisher's link is the only one.

The confidence labels in section 1 work the same way throughout: evidenced means research backs it, practitioner consensus means at least two published practitioner sources do it or recommend it, but nobody's studied it properly, and my view means it's my judgement, said plainly so you can disagree with it.
