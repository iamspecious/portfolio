# People Practice Playbook, Vol. 6: Pregnancy, Pregnancy Loss and Fertility at Work

Specious Coda-Bishop · Version 0.1.1 · Draft · 24 November 2026

## 1. Who this is for, and what it isn't

*Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 21 November 2026.*

**Who it's for:** everyone. This section tells you where to start.

If someone on your team has just told you they're pregnant, or quietly asked for a few mornings off for "appointments", or come back after a loss nobody at work knew about, and you're not sure what good looks like, this is for you. I'm writing for founders, People and HR leads, line managers and colleagues, and for the people living through it.

It covers trying to conceive, fertility treatment, fertility preservation, being pregnant at work, and every way a pregnancy can end: miscarriage, ectopic and molar pregnancy, stillbirth, neonatal death, termination for medical reasons or for any other reason, embryo transfers that don't lead to a pregnancy, and loss in a surrogacy. It covers coming back to work after a loss. It stops where parental leave begins. Leave after a birth, returning after a birth, feeding at work and childcare belong in a separate volume.

It's for anyone who is pregnant, trying to be, or affected by a pregnancy, whatever their gender identity or sexuality. That includes partners and non-carrying parents, surrogates, intended parents, people doing it on their own, and adoptive parents where their path overlaps with this one, such as a fertility history or an adoption match that falls through.

Two ideas run through the whole volume. Both are my position, not research findings.

**Support never depends on telling anyone.** Many people won't have told anyone at work yet, and some never will. Good support works without anyone having to explain themselves.

**No judgement, in either direction.** Whatever someone feels about a pregnancy, a loss, a termination or fertility treatment belongs to them. It might be grief, relief, both, neither, or something that shifts over months or years. It might not be what you'd feel in their place, and it doesn't need to be. In my experience, when we bring our own feelings into supporting someone, they tend to harden into a verdict, often without our meaning it to: they're taking it too hard, not hard enough, they should be past it by now, they shouldn't feel relieved. Support built on a verdict stops being support. So this volume describes what happened and what helps, never what anyone should feel, and nothing in it is timed, measured or graded. When someone comes to you, the aim is that they meet no judgement at all.

That doesn't mean ignoring someone who tells you they're struggling. Section 5 covers what to do then.

### Does any of this sound familiar?

These are illustrative, not real companies, but you'll probably recognise at least one.

- You're the founder of a 25-person company. Someone has asked whether you have a pregnancy loss policy. You don't, and you've never had a reason to think about it.
- You're the People lead at a 300-person company. One person on a team is going through IVF, another has just announced a pregnancy, and the team wants to throw a baby shower.
- You're a manager. Someone on your team keeps needing mornings off at short notice and hasn't said why. You've been told to "keep an eye on attendance".
- You're living with this. Your partner had a miscarriage last week. You weren't the one who was pregnant, and you don't know whether you're allowed to take time off.

Each of those needs something different, and the sections below are how to give it to them.

### Find your way in

- **Founders and leaders:** start with section 2, on what the evidence shows, then section 9 on how you'd know it's working.
- **People and HR leads:** section 4 sets out the choices, section 6 shows how three organisations made them, and section 10 has templates.
- **Managers:** section 5 is the conversation, and section 7 is the bias checks.
- **Colleagues:** section 5, especially the parts on who gets told what and what not to say.
- **If this is happening to you:** section 8 is written for you. You can skip everything else.

### How sure I am

The evidence is thin. A 2025 review of research on workplace support after early pregnancy loss found little empirical work, and every study with data came from high-income, English-speaking countries ([Kelly-Harrington et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/10519815241305007)). I found no research focused on trans or non-binary people who are pregnant at work. Where there's evidence, I cite it. Where there isn't, I say so, and anything that's my own view is labelled as mine.

### What this isn't

It isn't medical advice. Clinical bodies are used only to describe what something is, never to tell anyone what to do about it.

It isn't legal advice either. Leave, pay and protection around pregnancy depend heavily on where you are, and sometimes on how far along a pregnancy was. Where the law is likely to matter, this volume says so. Check what applies where you are.

### Related volumes

This volume stands on its own. If you need them, Vol. 5 covers menstruation and menopause, including conditions such as endometriosis and PCOS. Vol. 4 covers safety plans, and section 5 here points to it if someone isn't safe. A later volume will cover testosterone decline.

## 2. Why it matters, and where the popular numbers come from

*Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 21 November 2026.*

**Who it's for:** founders, leaders and People leads deciding whether this needs their attention.

The research is small. What there is shows that many people don't tell their manager or HR, and that people value being able to decide for themselves about time off and who knows.

### What the research shows

**Many people don't tell their manager or HR.** A 2025 review reports a 2022 survey of 295 UK employees by the CIPD, a professional body for HR: 24% told neither their manager nor HR. The same review gives 17% as telling nobody at work. Privacy, career worries and stigma were among the reasons people gave ([Kelly-Harrington et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/10519815241305007)). In a survey of 220 Australian men after a pregnancy loss, those whose loss came before 20 weeks were the least likely to tell their employer ([Obst et al., 2022](https://eres.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/bitstream/2440/129400/3/hdl_129400.pdf)).

**Hiding it has a cost.** In interviews with 18 people working in UK universities, many disguised fertility treatment as sick leave or a general medical appointment, and found the lying distressing. Arrangements agreed informally were fragile, and people worried they'd be lost if their manager changed ([Wilkinson, Mumford and Carroll, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/gwao.70136)).

**Control matters, because feelings are hard to predict.** Thirteen Australian women interviewed after an early pregnancy loss valued being able to decide for themselves, partly because they couldn't predict how they'd feel. Almost all felt some pressure to come back before they were ready, often unspoken. None used paid bereavement leave, and some didn't know it existed ([Donaghy et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/13668803.2024.2350550)).

**Not everyone wants all the leave on offer.** In a survey of 220 Australian men after a pregnancy loss or neonatal death, 91% told their workplace, and of those, 74% were offered leave. Some found work a helpful routine and didn't want all the leave offered. Others felt pressure to come back ([Obst et al., 2022](https://eres.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/bitstream/2440/129400/3/hdl_129400.pdf)).

**A written policy may help.** In a UK survey of 563 people combining work and fertility treatment, 23% had a workplace policy on treatment. Having a policy, and support from colleagues and their employer, went with lower distress about treatment. Telling someone at work, on its own, didn't. This shows a link, not that the policy caused it. Nearly all participants were women (98%) and white (95%), recruited through a fertility charity ([Payne, Seenan and van den Akker, 2019](https://mdx-repository.prod-uk.cayuse.com/download/75cbfc06587df066e4dc2fc45663768290a8ed2f578659ccae40259a2047e8dc/579768/FNUK_and_work_version_4.pdf)).

**LGBTQ+ parents fall through the gaps.** Twelve LGBTQ+ people interviewed after a pregnancy loss, as gestational and non-gestational parents, described having to navigate discriminatory policies, leave and entitlements ([Rose and Oxlad, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/13668803.2021.2020727)). A 2026 review of LGBTQ+ experiences of loss reports one person whose HR team refused leave because neither partner had been pregnant ([Darwin et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1732197)).

Every study with data in the 2025 review came from high-income, English-speaking countries ([Kelly-Harrington et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/10519815241305007)), and the 2026 LGBTQ+ review found only studies from the Global North, with just one focused on trans men and non-binary people ([Darwin et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1732197)). I found no research focused on trans or non-binary people who are pregnant at work. If you know of studies that fill these gaps, the changelog says how to send them.

## 3. Plain descriptions

*Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 24 November 2026.*

**Who it's for:** anyone who wants to know what the words mean, at work-level detail. This isn't medical information about what to do. For that, people talk to their own clinician.

I'm not a clinician. Where a description below is cited, it comes from a clinical body and covers only what helps you understand the work side: what it is, and why it might mean time off, appointments or follow-up. Where there's no citation, it's a plain description of how I understand the term, not a clinical definition. I've kept formal definitions to a minimum on purpose, because some clinical wording can be hard to read for the people it describes (see "Words first").

### Words first

The words people meet in clinic letters can land hard at work. The US National Library of Medicine's MedlinePlus lists "spontaneous abortion" as another name for miscarriage, alongside terms such as "missed abortion" and "incomplete abortion" ([MedlinePlus: Miscarriage](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001488.htm)). Someone may have read those words on their own paperwork.

My take: use the words the person uses. If they say "loss", say loss. If they say "termination", say termination. If they don't name it at all, you don't need to either. Say "pregnant person" or "carrying parent" unless someone tells you the word they'd like.

### Fertility

**Infertility.** The World Health Organization defines infertility as not becoming pregnant after 12 months or more of regular unprotected sex. It can come from male, female or unexplained factors. WHO names same-sex partners, people not in sexual relationships and cancer survivors among those who may need fertility care ([WHO, 2025](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/infertility)).

**Fertility treatment.** Treatment often involves IVF and other kinds of medically assisted reproduction ([WHO, 2025](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/infertility)). At work, that means appointments that can come at short notice and repeat in cycles. In one UK survey of 563 people, participants took an average of 8.74 days off per treatment cycle. Nearly all were women, recruited through a fertility charity ([Payne, Seenan and van den Akker, 2019](https://mdx-repository.prod-uk.cayuse.com/download/75cbfc06587df066e4dc2fc45663768290a8ed2f578659ccae40259a2047e8dc/579768/FNUK_and_work_version_4.pdf)).

**Fertility preservation.** Freezing and storing sperm, eggs, embryos or reproductive tissue for possible future use. People do it before medical treatment that could affect their fertility, and for non-medical reasons, such as wanting to have children later. The Royal College of Nursing's guidance names transgender people among those it may be relevant to ([Royal College of Nursing, 2024](https://rcn.org.uk/-/media/Royal-College-Of-Nursing/Documents/Publications/2024/October/011-754.pdf)). Egg freezing takes about two to four weeks and involves fertility medicines ([ESHRE: Egg freezing](https://eshre.eu/-/media/sitecore-files/SIGs/GSCA/IRHEC/Leaflets/ESHRE---Egg-freezing.pdf)). Whether the reason is medical or not, this volume treats it the same way.

### Pregnancy at work

Pregnancy brings antenatal appointments, and symptoms that vary from person to person. This volume doesn't describe them or give advice about them. **Legal flag:** many countries have specific rules on time off for antenatal care and on health and safety for pregnant workers. Check what applies where you are.

### How a pregnancy can end

This list isn't complete. It covers the endings named in the research and published policies this volume draws on. If someone's experience isn't here, it's covered all the same: support doesn't depend on finding the right category.

**Miscarriage.** The loss of a pregnancy before it reaches the stage counted as stillbirth. MedlinePlus, a US source, puts that line at 20 weeks ([MedlinePlus: Miscarriage](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001488.htm)). Other countries draw it elsewhere (see stillbirth, below).

**Ectopic pregnancy.** A pregnancy outside the womb, most often in a fallopian tube. It can't continue to birth and may be life threatening. Treatment means ending the pregnancy with medicine or surgery, and can mean repeat scans and blood tests ([MedlinePlus: Ectopic pregnancy](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000895.htm)).

**Molar pregnancy.** A rare growth that forms in the womb at the start of a pregnancy, from an egg that was fertilised abnormally. It's usually removed with a procedure, and hormone levels are then followed up for some months ([MedlinePlus: Hydatidiform mole](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000909.htm)). That follow-up can mean appointments long after the pregnancy has ended.

**Stillbirth.** A baby born with no signs of life after a certain point in pregnancy. WHO defines it from 22 weeks, but recommends 28 weeks for comparing countries, and many high-income countries count it from 20 or 22 weeks ([WHO Partnership for Maternal, Newborn and Child Health](https://pmnch.who.int/resources/tools-and-toolkits/stillbirths-toolkit/kenya/what-is-stillbirth)). **Legal flag:** where that line sits often changes what leave and pay someone gets. Check locally, and see section 4 for why an employer might choose not to draw a line at all.

**Neonatal death.** The death of a baby in the first weeks after birth.

**Termination of pregnancy.** Ending a pregnancy with medicine or a procedure. Some terminations happen because of a serious health concern for the baby or the pregnant person, often called termination for medical reasons. Others happen for any other reason. This volume covers both, and doesn't rank them. **Legal flag:** the law on termination varies widely between countries.

**Embryo transfer that doesn't lead to a pregnancy.** During fertility treatment, an embryo is placed in the womb but no pregnancy follows.

**Loss in a surrogacy.** Any of the above, when the pregnancy is carried by a surrogate. It can affect the surrogate and the intended parents.

### Feelings vary

There is no single way people feel about any of this. In interviews with 13 Australian women after an early pregnancy loss, people valued being able to decide for themselves partly because they couldn't predict how they'd feel ([Donaghy et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/13668803.2024.2350550)). A US study followed 667 people for five years after an abortion. The share who felt it was the right decision was 97.5% at the start and 99.0% at five years, and relief was the emotion most often reported, though some also reported sadness or guilt. Finding the decision hard, and sensing stigma, went with feeling less sure it was right years later. The study recruited at US clinics that provide abortions later in pregnancy ([Rocca et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2019.112704)).

None of this predicts how any one person will feel, after any kind of ending. What someone feels belongs to them, and it isn't ours to predict or judge (section 1).

## 4. What helps, and the choices

*Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 24 November 2026.*

**Who it's for:** People and HR leads, founders, and anyone writing or reviewing a policy.

The research supports a short list of things that help. Beyond that, there are real choices with trade-offs, and no single right answer. The first part below is the evidence; the second is the choices, with examples of how three organisations have handled them (section 6 covers those policies in full).

### What the research points to

**A written policy.** Having a workplace policy on fertility treatment, and support from colleagues and the employer, went with lower distress about treatment in a UK survey of 563 people. It shows a link, not that the policy caused it ([Payne, Seenan and van den Akker, 2019](https://mdx-repository.prod-uk.cayuse.com/download/75cbfc06587df066e4dc2fc45663768290a8ed2f578659ccae40259a2047e8dc/579768/FNUK_and_work_version_4.pdf)).

**Write down what's agreed.** People with informal arrangements worried they'd lose them if their manager changed ([Wilkinson, Mumford and Carroll, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/gwao.70136)). My take: a short written record, held by the person, protects what was agreed. Section 10 has a template.

**Let the person decide.** People valued being able to decide about time off and returning, partly because they couldn't predict how they'd feel ([Donaghy et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/13668803.2024.2350550)). Some men wanted less leave than they were offered; others felt pushed back too soon ([Obst et al., 2022](https://eres.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/bitstream/2440/129400/3/hdl_129400.pdf)). My take: the same support, offered on the person's terms, covers both.

**Make appointments easy to take without explaining.** People going through fertility treatment disguised appointments as sickness or general medical appointments, and found the lying distressing ([Wilkinson, Mumford and Carroll, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/gwao.70136)). My take: treat these like any other medical appointment, with the same leave and no reason needed.

**Include partners and every kind of family.** Men in one survey described needing time to be with and support their partner ([Obst et al., 2022](https://eres.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/bitstream/2440/129400/3/hdl_129400.pdf)). LGBTQ+ parents interviewed after a pregnancy loss described having to navigate discriminatory policies, leave and entitlements ([Rose and Oxlad, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/13668803.2021.2020727)). My take: name partners, non-carrying parents, surrogates and intended parents in the policy itself, so nobody has to argue their way in.

**Keep it off the attendance record.** My take: absence linked to fertility treatment, pregnancy or a pregnancy ending shouldn't count towards attendance warnings. In the UK interview study above, one person's absences for treatment led to formal attendance management ([Wilkinson, Mumford and Carroll, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/gwao.70136)). That's one case, not a pattern the research measured.

### The choices

There's no single right answer to any of these. Each organisation picks what fits, and can change its mind. The last column shows how other organisations have handled each choice. They're examples to learn from, not a standard to match or a ranking. The "What it offers" and "What to watch" columns are my take.

## 5. The conversation

*Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 24 November 2026.*

**Who it's for:** managers, People and HR, and colleagues.

Most of this section is my take, because research on what to say is thin. Where a study backs something, it's cited.

### They lead

You'll never know everything someone is carrying, so don't guess. Let them choose what to tell you, when, and how much. If they tell you nothing beyond "I need some time off", that's enough.

People have real reasons to hold back. Women interviewed after an early pregnancy loss described weighing up the unwritten "12-week rule" about announcing a pregnancy, fear of being seen as less capable, and wanting privacy ([Donaghy et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/13668803.2024.2350550)). One study included in a 2025 review describes people feeling pressure to "be professional" after a loss ([Kelly-Harrington et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/10519815241305007)).

When someone does tell you, a good first response is short: thank them for telling you, ask what would help right now, and ask who else, if anyone, they want to know.

### Telling people about a pregnancy

Telling people at work is rarely one conversation. A study of 92 women describes it as an ongoing process involving many people, where they chose when it felt safe to tell, told some people and not others, and tried to manage how and where the news came out ([Verhoff and Buzzanell, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/10510974.2022.2097720)).

My take: let the person decide the order and the timing. Don't announce on their behalf, and don't ask "when are you going to tell the team?"

### When the news changes

Sometimes a pregnancy people know about ends, or treatment stops, or plans change. The person may now face colleagues asking how the pregnancy is going.

My take: ask them how they'd like it handled. Some want a short message sent for them, so they don't have to explain it again and again. Some want nothing said. Some want to tell people themselves. Section 10 has a template for agreeing who gets told what.

### Who gets told what

My take: nothing about a pregnancy, a loss, fertility treatment or anything connected to it goes to anyone the person hasn't explicitly said can know. That includes partners, family, colleagues and your own manager. Things can change quickly, so what was fine to share last month may not be fine now. If you're not sure, ask first.

If you need to tell someone to arrange cover or pay, tell them only what they need ("X is on leave until Monday"), and tell the person what you've shared.

### Coming back to work

There's no right time and no right way to come back. People in the research valued being able to decide for themselves ([Donaghy et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/13668803.2024.2350550)). Some men found work a helpful routine, while others found returning hard ([Obst et al., 2022](https://eres.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/bitstream/2440/129400/3/hdl_129400.pdf)).

My take: before they come back, ask what they'd like their first days to look like, and what, if anything, colleagues should know. For some people, certain dates or moments can be hard, such as a due date, an anniversary, or a colleague's pregnancy news. Others won't have any of these. Don't assume either way. If they tell you about one, believe them and make room for it.

### If someone tells you they're struggling

Not judging how someone feels doesn't mean ignoring them when they say they're finding things hard. My take: listen, ask what would help, and make sure they know what support exists, such as flexibility, leave, or any counselling or assistance programme your organisation offers. You aren't there to diagnose or counsel. If you're worried about their immediate safety, contact emergency services.

### What not to say

My take, from the principle in section 1. These all pass a judgement, even when they're meant kindly:

- "At least it was early."
- "You can always try again."
- "Everything happens for a reason."
- "Are you sure you're ready to be back?"
- "You seem to be coping really well."
- "Why did you need the time off?"
- Any comment on a termination decision, either way.

If you don't know what to say, "I'm sorry, and I'm here if you need anything" is enough. So is asking what would help.

### If someone isn't safe

Violence from a partner during pregnancy has drawn the attention of the World Health Organization ([WHO, 2011](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/WHO-RHR-11.35)). Abuse can also be about pregnancy itself. A systematic review describes reproductive coercion as behaviour that gets in the way of someone's own decisions about reproductive health, including pressure to get pregnant or control over how a pregnancy ends ([Grace and Anderson, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/1524838016663935)). If someone tells you they're not safe:

1. Listen, and believe them.
2. Connect them with help, including specialist support services where you are.
3. Don't investigate, and don't contact the person they're worried about. If that person works for your organisation, talk to HR about what to do next, and check what applies where you are.
4. If anyone is in immediate danger, contact emergency services.

Vol. 4 covers safety plans in full, including practical steps at work. It's the one place this volume points elsewhere, because safety matters more than keeping each volume separate.

## 6. Published examples

*Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 24 November 2026.*

**Who it's for:** anyone writing or reviewing a policy who wants to see how others have done it.

Pregnancy, fertility treatment and pregnancy loss are part of working life, and stigma and silence get in the way of people getting the support they need (section 2). Seeing how other organisations have written this down helps make it ordinary. Three publish their policies openly: a UK company, an Irish university and a UK broadcaster. They're examples to learn from, not a ranking. For each, I've set out what it does well, then what I'd do differently and why. That's my take, not a verdict on the people who wrote them. None of the three states a licence, so read them at the links rather than copying them.

All three are written under UK or Irish law. Where a policy relies on a legal rule, I note it but don't compare the legal detail. Check what applies where you are.

### PayCaptain: Fertility Support, and Pregnancy Loss and Bereavement

PayCaptain is a UK company that publishes an open handbook. Its [Fertility Support policy](https://handbook.paycaptain.com/fertility) was last reviewed on 16 May 2024. Its [Pregnancy Loss and Bereavement policy](https://handbook.paycaptain.com/benefits/health/miscarriage-policy) is undated. Both were read on 20 November 2026.

**What it does well.**

- The loss policy defines a wide range of losses, including embryo transfers that don't lead to a pregnancy and termination, and names a surrogate carrying the employee's baby from its opening lines.
- Absence after a loss before 24 weeks is recorded as pregnancy-related, so it doesn't count towards sickness records, attendance warnings, or decisions about someone's job, promotion or redundancy.
- Partners get the same 10 days of paid leave as the person who was pregnant.
- It gives a template for asking for leave that describes it as a personal health matter and asks to keep the details private.
- The manager asks how much, if anything, the person wants colleagues to know.
- It lists practical changes for coming back, such as later start times, more breaks and turning the camera off on video calls.
- The fertility policy gives paid time off for appointments, tests and procedures, and counselling for employees and their partners.

**What I'd do differently.**

- The loss policy defines termination for any reason, but its section on protected leave names only termination for medical reasons. I'd say clearly whether all terminations are covered.
- The leave is described as bereavement leave "to grieve and recover". I'd use a name that doesn't assume what someone feels (see the choices in section 4).
- The leave template names the pregnancy-related leave policy, which tells the manager what the leave is for. I'd also offer a version that doesn't, so nobody has to say more than they want to.
- The fertility policy encourages people to feel comfortable discussing fertility with their managers and colleagues. That's well meant, but I'd say who someone tells is entirely their choice.
- The fertility policy doesn't mention partners' time off for appointments, fertility preservation, surrogacy, or routes to parenthood for LGBTQ+ people and people doing it alone. I'd name them.
- The loss policy asks for a doctor's note after seven days and refers to UK equality law. That's a legal point, so check what applies where you are.

### Dublin City University: Pregnancy Loss and Fertility Leave Policy

DCU is a university in Ireland. Its [Pregnancy Loss and Fertility Leave Policy](https://www.dcu.ie/policies/pregnancy-loss-fertility-leave-policy) (version 1.0, approved 23 June 2026) comes with [guidance for managers](https://www.dcu.ie/people/benefits/pregnancy-loss-fertility-support-guidance-for-managers) (page updated 11 November 2026). Both were read on 20 November 2026.

**What it does well.**

- It applies to all genders and to partners. On fertility, it includes same-sex couples, people seeking to have a child on their own, and surrogacy.
- Its manager guidance says a neutral, accepting or relieved response is as valid as grief. That matches the principle in section 1.
- Leave codes are visible only to the employee, their manager and the People team, and nothing is shared without the employee's written consent.
- It sets clear amounts: up to 10 paid days after a loss before 24 weeks, and up to 10 days per fertility treatment cycle, with up to 30 in total.

**What I'd do differently.**

- It lists abortion as a pregnancy loss in its definitions, but its leave section doesn't say whether abortion is covered. I'd make that explicit.
- Partners don't get the paid days after a loss. They're offered counselling and existing leave options. I'd give partners the same leave (see section 4).
- It asks for a certificate from a GP or consultant. I'd consider self-certification for at least the first days, so people don't need paperwork to take leave (see section 4).
- A cap of 30 fertility days is clear and predictable. I'd add how someone can ask for more if treatment runs longer, so the cap isn't read as the limit of support.

### Channel 4: Pregnancy Loss Policy

Channel 4 is a UK broadcaster. Its [Pregnancy Loss Policy](https://assets-corporate.channel4.com/_flysystem/s3/2021-04/C4%20Pregnancy%20Loss%20Policy%20April%202021%20-%20Final.pdf) is dated April 2021 and was read on 20 November 2026. It covers pregnancy loss only, not fertility treatment.

**What it does well.**

- It covers people affected directly, through a partner, or through a surrogate carrying their baby, from their first day.
- It lists abortion as a loss, with no limit on the reason. Of the three, it's the only one that leaves no doubt about termination.
- It gives at least two weeks' paid leave, self-certified, with no doctor's note.
- It says recovery has no time limit, and that it makes no assumptions about how people feel or want to be treated.
- It says pregnancy loss isn't limited to women or straight couples.
- It offers paid time off for related appointments, a trained buddy and confidential contacts beyond the manager, and requires manager training.

**What I'd do differently.**

- It encourages people to tell their line manager early and keep them informed. That's meant to get support in place, but I'd say who someone tells, and when, is entirely their choice.
- Embryo transfers that don't lead to a pregnancy aren't named. I'd add them.
- After a stillbirth, it refers to "female employees carrying the pregnancy". That wording follows UK maternity law, but I'd use gender-neutral wording wherever the law allows.
- It's from 2021. I'd review it against newer research, such as the 2025 review cited in section 2.

### What they have in common

All three name partners and surrogacy, and all three go beyond saying "talk to your manager". The gap that comes up most is termination: two of the three define it as a loss but leave it unclear in their leave rules. If you're writing a policy, that's the place to be explicit.

## 7. Bias checks

*Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 24 November 2026.*

**Who it's for:** anyone writing a policy, managing someone, or deciding who gets what.

These are the assumptions most likely to leave someone out or treat them unfairly. Each check says what it looks like and what to do instead. Where research backs the check, it's cited; the rest is my take.

### Who counts as typical

**Assuming the woman is the one with fertility problems.** WHO notes that women in a relationship with a man are often assumed to be the infertile partner, whether they are or not, and that infertility can come from male, female or unexplained factors ([WHO, 2025](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/infertility)). *Instead:* make fertility support open to anyone, including men having their own fertility checked, and don't ask whose "problem" it is.

**Assuming a straight couple.** WHO names same-sex partners and people not in sexual relationships among those who may need fertility care ([WHO, 2025](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/infertility)). LGBTQ+ parents interviewed after a pregnancy loss described having to navigate discriminatory policies, leave and entitlements ([Rose and Oxlad, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/13668803.2021.2020727)). *Instead:* write policies for partners, non-carrying parents, surrogates, intended parents and people doing it alone, by name.

**Assuming only the person who was pregnant was affected.** A 2026 review reports one person whose HR team refused leave because neither partner had been pregnant ([Darwin et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1732197)). *Instead:* cover everyone affected by the pregnancy, not only the person carrying it.

**Assuming everyone who is pregnant is a woman.** I found no research focused on trans or non-binary people who are pregnant at work, and only one study in the LGBTQ+ review focused on trans men and non-binary people ([Darwin et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1732197)). No research doesn't mean no people. *Instead:* use gender-neutral wording wherever the law allows, and don't assume anyone's gender from their pregnancy, or their pregnancy from their gender.

### Ranking experiences

**Ranking losses by how far along the pregnancy was.** In a survey of 220 Australian men after a pregnancy loss, those whose loss came before 20 weeks were the least likely to tell their employer ([Obst et al., 2022](https://eres.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/bitstream/2440/129400/3/hdl_129400.pdf)). The study doesn't say why. My take: rules that give less support for earlier losses tell people an early loss counts for less. *Instead:* offer the same support regardless of how far along the pregnancy was. **Legal flag:** some legal rights change at a set number of weeks. Check what applies where you are.

**Ranking endings by the reason.** My take: treating a termination as less deserving of support than a miscarriage, or a termination for medical reasons as more deserving than one for any other reason, is a judgement this volume doesn't make. *Instead:* support doesn't depend on why a pregnancy ended, and nobody has to say.

**Judging how someone is coping.** See section 1. *Instead:* don't comment on whether someone is too upset, not upset enough, or back too soon. Ask what would help.

### How people are judged at work

**Letting pregnancy or treatment change how someone is assessed.** My take: quietly moving someone off a project, passing them over for a stretch opportunity, or assuming they'll leave, because they're pregnant or trying, is a bias, however kindly meant. *Instead:* ask them what they want, and assess the work, not the circumstances. **Legal flag:** many countries protect against pregnancy discrimination. Check what applies where you are.

**Treating being seen at work as proof of commitment.** People going through fertility treatment disguised appointments as sickness ([Wilkinson, Mumford and Carroll, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/gwao.70136)). My take: where presence counts as commitment, people hide what they need. *Instead:* judge the work, not the hours seen at a desk.

### Who the research is about

**Assuming the research describes everyone.** Every study with data in the 2025 review came from high-income, English-speaking countries ([Kelly-Harrington et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/10519815241305007)). In one fertility survey, 98% of participants were women and 95% were white ([Payne, Seenan and van den Akker, 2019](https://mdx-repository.prod-uk.cayuse.com/download/75cbfc06587df066e4dc2fc45663768290a8ed2f578659ccae40259a2047e8dc/579768/FNUK_and_work_version_4.pdf)). *Instead:* treat the findings in this volume as a starting point, and ask the people in your organisation what they need.

## 8. For you

*Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 24 November 2026.*

**Who it's for:** you, if this is happening to you. Read the part that fits and skip the rest. Most of this is my take, written to you directly.

### Wherever you're starting from

- You don't have to tell anyone at work why you need time off. "A medical appointment", "a personal health matter" or "a family matter" is enough.
- You choose who knows, and you can change your mind. That might be your manager, someone in HR, or someone else you trust.
- If you agree something informally, such as flexible hours or time for appointments, ask for it in writing. People in one study worried informal arrangements would be lost if their manager changed ([Wilkinson, Mumford and Carroll, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/gwao.70136)). Section 10 has a template you can use.
- Whatever you feel is yours. There's no right amount, and no right time.

### If you're pregnant

You decide when to tell people, in what order, and how. If you'd rather not have the news marked at work, or want it marked a certain way, say so. If you need time for antenatal appointments, check what your organisation and the law where you are give you.

### If you're a partner or a non-carrying parent

This affects you too. Men in one study described needing time to be with and support their partner ([Obst et al., 2022](https://eres.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/bitstream/2440/129400/3/hdl_129400.pdf)), and a 2026 review reports one LGBTQ+ parent refused leave because neither partner had been pregnant ([Darwin et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1732197)). If your organisation's policy doesn't name you, it's still worth asking. The examples in section 6 all mention partners, though not all give them the same leave.

### If you're a surrogate

You're pregnant, and everything in this volume applies to you, including if the pregnancy ends, and in the weeks after the birth. A 2025 review includes an employer case study noting that policies need to say clearly that they cover surrogacy ([Kelly-Harrington et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/10519815241305007)), so you may need to ask directly what leave and support you can have.

### If you're an intended parent

You may be going through treatment, waiting, a pregnancy someone else is carrying, or a loss you weren't physically part of. All of these can need time and support. Ask what your organisation's policy covers. If it's silent, section 4 sets out what you could ask for.

### If you're doing this on your own

Policies are often written with couples in mind. WHO names people not in sexual relationships among those who may need fertility care ([WHO, 2025](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/infertility)). You have the same claim to support as anyone else.

### If you're trying, in treatment, or preserving your fertility

Treatment can mean appointments at short notice, in cycles, often over months ([Payne, Seenan and van den Akker, 2019](https://mdx-repository.prod-uk.cayuse.com/download/75cbfc06587df066e4dc2fc45663768290a8ed2f578659ccae40259a2047e8dc/579768/FNUK_and_work_version_4.pdf)). It's a medical appointment like any other, and you don't need to explain it.

That includes checking your own fertility, whatever your gender. Infertility can come from male, female or unexplained factors ([WHO, 2025](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/infertility)), and anyone who produces sperm can have their fertility checked. A separate, later volume covers testosterone decline.

It also includes fertility preservation, whether it's before medical treatment or for any other reason. This volume treats both the same way.

### After a pregnancy ends

However it ended, and however you feel about it, you're entitled to the same care. If it's grief, it might be a lot at first. If it's relief, that's real too. It might be both, neither, or something that changes.

*My take:* Think of a young tree with a cut in its bark. At first, the mark takes up a big part of the trunk. As the tree keeps growing, the mark stays, but more and more of the tree grows around it. That's not a timeline or a promise, and the mark might still catch you on some days. But with time and growth, there may be longer stretches where it doesn't. This is close to a model the grief counsellor Lois Tonkin called "growing around grief" ([Cruse](https://www.cruse.org.uk/?p=696)). If none of this fits how you feel, that's fine too. Not everyone has a mark to grow around.

When you're ready to come back, you can ask for what you need: a phased return, changes to your hours, no announcement, or a short message sent for you. Section 10 has a template.

### If you're not safe

If someone is pressuring you about whether to get pregnant, continue a pregnancy, end one, or go through treatment, or if you're being hurt, you deserve support. Tell someone you trust, at work or outside it. If you're in immediate danger, contact emergency services. Vol. 4 covers safety plans in full, including steps you can take at work.

### If you're the manager, and this is happening to you

You're entitled to the same support you'd give your team. You don't have to tell your team, or even your own manager, more than you want to. If you need cover, someone in HR or another manager can arrange it without everyone knowing why.

## 9. Knowing whether it works

*Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 24 November 2026.*

**Who it's for:** founders, leaders and People leads.

You can tell whether support is working without collecting anyone's health information or measuring how they feel. My take: measure whether support is easy to find, easy to use and holds up over time. Never measure grief, wellbeing or recovery, because that's the judgement this volume avoids.

| What to check | Why | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Do people know what's available? | Some women interviewed after a loss didn't know paid bereavement leave existed ([Donaghy et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/13668803.2024.2350550)) | An anonymous question in an existing survey: "Do you know what support is available for fertility treatment, pregnancy and pregnancy loss?" |
| Is it easy to ask? | People hold back for privacy, career and stigma reasons ([Kelly-Harrington et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/10519815241305007)) | An anonymous question: "If you needed this support, how easy would it be to ask for?" |
| Do arrangements survive change? | People worried informal arrangements would be lost when managers changed ([Wilkinson, Mumford and Carroll, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/gwao.70136)) | When someone changes manager, check that written arrangements carried over. Count only whether they did, not what they were |
| Is the leave being used, and by whom? | Use shows the policy is real; low use by partners may mean they don't feel included | Totals per leave type each year, including partners. Don't report small numbers that could identify someone |
| Is anyone being penalised? | Related absence shouldn't count towards attendance warnings (section 4) | Check that no attendance warning was triggered by fertility, pregnancy or pregnancy-loss leave |

**Keep it private.** My take: report only totals, never individuals, and leave out any figure small enough to point to a person. In a small team, even "one person used pregnancy loss leave this year" can identify someone. **Legal flag:** health information often has extra legal protection. Check what applies where you are before recording or reporting anything.

## 10. Templates

*Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 24 November 2026.*

**Who it's for:** anyone who wants a starting point. Copy them, change them, and use your own words. None of them asks for medical details.

### 1. Asking for time off without giving details

```markdown
Hi [name],

I need some time off for a personal or family matter, from [date] for [number of days, or "I'm not sure yet"]. I'd prefer to keep the details private.

Could you let me know what I need to do to record it? I'll keep you posted about when I'm back.

Thanks,
[your name]
```

### 2. Who gets told what

For the person to fill in, and update whenever they like. Their manager or People contact keeps a copy only if the person wants them to.

```markdown
Who can know: [names or roles]
What they can know: [e.g. "I'm on leave", "I'm going through treatment", or more]
Who shouldn't be told anything: [names or roles, if any]
If my news changes, I'd like: [to tell people myself / a short message sent for me / nothing said]
The message, if one is sent: [the exact words]
Last updated: [date]
```

### 3. A record of what's agreed

So arrangements don't depend on one manager remembering them. The person keeps the original.

```markdown
Agreed between: [person] and [manager or People contact]
Date: [date]

What's agreed:
- Time off for appointments: [e.g. as needed, with as much notice as possible]
- Flexibility: [e.g. later starts, working from home, camera off on calls]
- How absence is recorded: [e.g. as a medical appointment, not sickness]
- Who to contact if things change: [name]

This carries over if either of us changes role. Either of us can ask to review it at any time.
```

### 4. Coming back to work

For before someone returns, after a pregnancy ends or after time off for treatment. Every line is optional.

```markdown
Return date: [date, or "to be agreed"]
How I'd like to come back: [e.g. full hours / reduced hours to start / phased over [weeks]]
What colleagues know: [nothing / that I've been on leave / more]
What would help in the first days: [e.g. no catch-up meetings on day one, a quieter workload]
Dates that may be hard, if any: [only if you want to share them]
Who to talk to if things change: [name]
```

### 5. A one-page guide for managers

```markdown
If someone tells you about a pregnancy, fertility treatment or a pregnancy ending:

- Thank them. Ask what would help, and who else, if anyone, they want to know.
- Don't ask for details or reasons. "A medical appointment" is enough.
- Share nothing with anyone they haven't named. If you need cover, share only what's needed.
- Don't assume how they feel, and don't comment on how they're coping.
- Offer to write down what you agree, and give them the copy.
- Keep related absence off attendance warnings.
- If they say they're not safe: listen, believe them, connect them with help, and don't investigate. If anyone is in immediate danger, contact emergency services.
- Check your organisation's policy and the law where you are.
```
