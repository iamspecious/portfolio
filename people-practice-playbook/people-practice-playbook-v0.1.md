# People Practice Playbook, Vol. 1: Job Architecture and Levelling

Specious Coda-Bishop · Version 0.1 · 6 November 2026

## 1. Who this is for, and what it isn't

*Version 0.1, draft. Section last reviewed 6 November 2026.*

If someone's asked you to "sort out our levels" and you're not sure where to start, this is for you. I'm writing for people at companies of roughly 50 to 500, usually in HR or L&D, or a manager who's ended up holding it. I'm assuming you don't have a consultant on speed dial or months to spare.

There's no shortage of ladders out there to copy. What there isn't much of is the working: how to figure out what's actually wrong, what to do first, and how to get anyone to use the thing once it exists. That's what this is.

The pattern I see most is people buying the wrong fix. The ladder is honestly fine, the real problem is that managers don't know how to use it, and the answer everyone lands on is more detail. Six months later the document is longer and fewer people open it. So the whole playbook starts from one idea: work out what problem you've got before you build anything.

### Does any of this sound familiar?

These are made up to illustrate, not real companies, but you'll probably recognise at least one.

- You're an engineering manager at a 120-person company. There's a ladder, nobody opens it, and two of your senior people just left saying they couldn't see where they were going. Leadership wants a more detailed ladder.
- You're the only HR person at a 300-person company and you've been asked to add levels for support. Support is eight people and has never had a track.
- You're in L&D and you've been handed the company values and told to turn them into career criteria. Nobody can tell you what "ownership" looks like on a normal Tuesday.
- You've inherited a ladder from the last revamp. Half your managers bring it into 1:1s. The other half have never mentioned it.

Every one of those is a different problem, and the fix for one can make another worse. Section 2 is how to tell which one you've got.

### What this isn't

It isn't a template. There are good free ones and I've listed them in section 10. It doesn't cover pay bands, performance reviews or hiring. Those are later volumes, and where levelling bumps into them I'll say so and leave it there.

It isn't legal advice either. If you're in the EU or you have a works council, check locally before you tie levels to pay.

And it isn't a big tech company's answer scaled down. A lot of what's published comes from places with conditions you probably don't have, and I'll point it out when that matters.

### How sure am I?

Everything in here is one of three kinds, and I've tried to make it obvious which without tagging every line. If there's a link, there's a source behind it, and the text says whether that's research or someone's published practice. If it's my view, I'll say so in plain words. Most writing on this topic presents someone's opinion as if it's research, and I'd rather you know which is which.

| Label | What it means | An example from this playbook |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Evidenced | There's research behind it, and it's cited | Feedback aimed at the person rather than the work can make performance worse |
| Practitioner consensus | At least two published practitioner sources do it or recommend it, and they're linked. Nobody's studied it properly | Describing levels by scope and autonomy rather than by tasks (SFIA and Dropbox both do this) |
| My view | A position I'm taking, and I'll say so | Diagnose before you design |

When the evidence is thin I'll tell you, even when that's most of a section. That's more use to you than me sounding confident. And if I can't point you to something published outside my own head, I'll tell you it's my view, full stop.

## 2. Diagnose first: which problem have you actually got?

*Section last reviewed 6 November 2026.*

When people say "our levels are a mess", they usually mean one of four different things. Each one needs a different fix, and the fix for one can make another worse. Spend a week here before you spend six months building. That's my view, and it's the one this whole playbook rests on.

### The four problems

| Problem | What you'll hear | What's really going on | What goes wrong if you treat it as a writing job |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Design | "I can't tell what the difference is between a 3 and a 4" | The ladder itself is unclear, or was written for one function and stretched over everyone | Usually nothing. This is the one problem a rewrite actually fixes |
| Adoption | "We have a ladder? I've never seen my manager use it" | The ladder's fine. Managers don't bring it into conversations, and decisions get made some other way | You make it longer and more detailed, and even fewer people open it |
| Prerequisite | "Depends who you ask what senior means here" | There's no shared idea of what good looks like. The values are on a wall, not in anyone's behaviour | You write criteria for something nobody's agreed on, and every calibration turns into an argument |
| Political | "This is really about \[one person / one team / one re-org\]" | The ladder is standing in for a fight about money, status or a decision someone wants backed up | Whatever you write gets bent to the outcome someone already wanted, and people can tell |

You can have more than one. From talking this through with other people working in and around HR, two at once is more common than one.

### Five quick checks

None of these need a survey tool or a budget. Each one takes an afternoon at most. They're checks I'd run, not tested methods.

1. **The two-manager test.** Write up one real person's recent work, with their name and level taken off. Give it to two managers who don't work closely together, along with the ladder, and ask each to place them separately. If they land more than a level apart, you've got a design or a prerequisite problem.
2. **The last-looked question.** Ask five people across different teams when they last opened the ladder and why. If the honest answer is "at my review" or "never", that's adoption.
3. **The one-sentence test.** Ask each member of the leadership team to describe what "senior" means here in one sentence, on their own. If you get five different answers, that's a prerequisite problem, and no amount of writing will fix it.
4. **Follow the last three promotions.** Did the ladder come up in the decision, or in the conversation with the person afterwards? If it didn't, either managers don't use it (adoption) or the decisions happen somewhere the ladder can't reach (political).
5. **Who asked, and why now?** Find out who asked for this work and what they expect to change. If the answer is about one person, one team or one upcoming re-org, treat it as political until you're sure it isn't.

### What order to fix them in

If you've got more than one, I'd go prerequisite first, then name the political problem out loud, then design, then adoption. Getting managers excited about a ladder that's broken burns trust you'll need later. Rewriting a ladder before leaders agree on what good looks like means rewriting it again.

The political one is the hardest to say. You don't have to solve it. You do have to name it to whoever asked for the work, or the rest of this playbook won't help you much.

### An example

This is illustrative, not a real company.

Remember the engineering manager from section 1? 120 people, a ladder nobody opens, two senior people gone. Leadership wants a more detailed ladder.

She runs the two-manager test on three people. All three times the managers land on the same level, so the writing isn't the problem. Then she follows the last three promotions. None of them mention the ladder. They were decided in a leadership channel and announced afterwards. When she asks people when they last looked at it, most say "when I joined".

That's adoption, with a bit of politics around how promotions get decided. A more detailed ladder would have taken months and changed nothing. What she actually needs is in section 6, and a frank conversation about where promotion decisions get made.

### How sure is this?

Nobody has tested this four-way split. It comes from patterns I keep seeing, and you should treat it that way.

There is research behind one part of it, though. Studies of how HR practices land in organisations keep finding a gap between what was designed and what managers actually do with it, and point to line managers as a major cause of that gap ([Kent](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/3202131)). A related idea says a system only works when people see it applied consistently and agree on what it means, not just when it's well written. Its own authors say that idea is still under-tested ([summary](https://programme.exordo.com/iam2018/delegates/presentation/134)).

**What would change my mind:** good evidence that companies which rewrote their ladder, without doing anything on adoption, saw better outcomes than companies that worked on adoption alone. If you've got that data, I want to see it.

## 3. Prerequisites: do you know what good looks like here?

*Section last reviewed 6 November 2026.*

A ladder describes what good looks like at each level. If your leaders haven't agreed what good looks like at all, the ladder has nothing to describe, and you'll end up writing their disagreements down and calling it a framework.

The groundwork runs in a chain:

1. **Values.** What the company actually cares about.
2. **Behaviours.** What that looks like when someone's doing it on an ordinary day.
3. **Competencies.** Those behaviours, grouped up and described at each level, which is what goes into the ladder.

Most companies have the first one written down somewhere. Very few have done the second. That gap is where most prerequisite problems live.

### Why a values page isn't enough

This is one of the better-evidenced bits of the whole playbook.

A study of S&P 500 companies found the values on their websites didn't line up with the values employees said they actually saw. Those advertised values had no link to how the companies performed. What did link to performance was whether employees saw their managers as trustworthy and ethical ([Guiso, Sapienza and Zingales](https://repec.cepr.org/repec/cpr/ceprdp/DP9716.pdf)).

There's a name for that gap. Researchers call it behavioural integrity: whether people see the words and the actions matching ([Simons](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ororsc:v:13:y:2002:i:1:p:18-35)). A survey of 343 employees in Australian organisations found commitment was higher when the stated values and the lived ones matched ([Howell, Kirk-Brown and Cooper, 2012](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09585192.2011.561251)).

This is why it matters for you. A ladder is one of the places where your values either show up in real decisions or get quietly found out. If the values say "collaboration" and the last three promotions went to people who shipped alone and steamrolled everyone, a ladder that says "collaboration" just makes the gap official.

### A useful way to sort your values

Patrick Lencioni splits values into four kinds ([HBR, 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12140851/)):

- **Core.** What's genuinely true of you now, even when it costs you.
- **Aspirational.** What you want to be true but isn't yet.
- **Permission-to-play.** The basics any company should meet, like honesty.
- **Accidental.** Things that crept in without anyone choosing them.

Only core values belong in a ladder as written. Aspirational ones can go in, but say so out loud, otherwise people get assessed against something the company doesn't do itself. Permission-to-play stuff usually belongs in a code of conduct, not a level. That's my read of Lencioni rather than research, and it's worth knowing his firm sells consulting on exactly this.

### Three checks before you write anything

No budget needed for these, and they're my own checks rather than tested methods.

1. **The last-month test.** For each value, can a leader name something a specific person did last month that showed it? If nobody can, it's a poster, not a value yet.
2. **The five-answers test.** Ask five leaders separately what one value looks like in practice. If you get five different answers, you need a conversation before you need a document.
3. **The promotion test.** Would anyone actually use this value to decide a promotion, or to turn one down? If not, it shouldn't be a ladder criterion, because nobody will treat it as one.

### An example

This is illustrative, not a real company.

Remember the L&D lead from section 1, handed the values and told to turn them into career criteria? One of the values is "ownership".

She asks five leaders what ownership looked like last month. She gets: "stays late to get things over the line", "fixes bugs nobody assigned", "pushes back when scope creeps", "doesn't need chasing", and "tells me early when something's slipping".

Some of those pull in opposite directions. Pushing back on scope and getting things over the line at any cost aren't the same value. And "stays late" rewards the people with the fewest commitments outside work, which isn't ownership, it's availability. Section 9 comes back to this, because it's exactly how a ladder ends up penalising carers without anyone meaning it to.

After one conversation, the leaders settle on something closer to this:

| Level | What ownership looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Early career | Says early when something's slipping, rather than hoping it sorts itself out |
| Mid | Fixes what's broken in their own area without being asked, and tells people what they did |
| Senior | Spots problems that fall between teams and makes sure someone owns them, which isn't always them |

That's three sentences. It took a 90-minute conversation to get there, and that conversation is the actual work.

### What about competency formats?

There's a lot of advice out there on how to format competencies: rating scales, behavioural anchors, matrices. The evidence on format is old and mixed. A 1975 study with US Navy officers found behaviourally anchored scales somewhat better than plain or trait-based scales on most of the measures tested ([Borman and Dunnette](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1148)). Another study the same year found they cut halo error but did nothing for leniency ([Keaveny and McGann](https://epublications.marquette.edu/mgmt_fac/306)). A comparison in two samples of technicians found them no better than simple global ratings on any measure ([abstract](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1300/J075v09n02_10)).

The most-cited set of best practices for competency modelling says openly that it's based on experience, not on research ([Campion et al., 2011](https://apps.it.purdue.edu/sites/Home/DirectoryApi/Files/0e3546ac-6f92-4520-bc41-d38da187f4e0/Download)). So if someone tells you their format is the evidence-based one, ask to see the evidence.

My read is that the conversation matters more than the template. Spend your time getting leaders to agree what the behaviours are, and keep the format as plain as you can get away with.

### If the groundwork isn't there

Don't write the ladder yet. As I see it, you've got two honest options.

- **Do the values-to-behaviours work first.** One conversation per value, using the checks above. It's slower, but everything after it goes faster.
- **Describe what you actually reward now.** Look at the last few promotions and write down, honestly, what got people there. Then decide whether you like it. It's less inspiring, but it's true, and it often starts the values conversation on its own.

**What would change my mind:** evidence that companies which built ladders straight from aspirational values, without the behaviour step, got the same results as companies that did the behaviour work first.

## 4. Design decisions: the trade-offs

*Section last reviewed 6 November 2026.*

There's no correct ladder design. There are six decisions, each with a cost either way, and the right call depends on your size, your functions and what problem you diagnosed in section 2. I'll give you the trade-off and say where I'd lean, but treat the leaning as mine.

| Decision | One way | The other way | Where I'd usually start at 50 to 500 people |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| How detailed | Short and general | Long and granular | Specific enough to measure, short enough to read in one sitting |
| What you describe | Competencies (behaviours) | Skills (things you can do) | Competencies for levels, skills for development conversations |
| How many ladders | One spine for everyone | A ladder per function | One spine, plus dedicated ladders only for your biggest functions |
| Management | IC and management on one track | Separate, parallel tracks | Parallel tracks once you have senior ICs worth keeping, not before |
| What a level means | Tasks or years | Scope and autonomy | Scope and autonomy |
| How many levels | Few, wide levels | Many, narrow levels | More levels early in careers, fewer at the top |

### 1. How detailed should it be?

Too vague and nobody can tell a 3 from a 4. Too detailed and it turns into a checklist nobody reads, or worse, one people game.

Two published sources pull in the same direction here. Camille Fournier, who published one of the first public engineering ladders at Rent the Runway, says a ladder should be more prescriptive than a horoscope, with rough ways to measure fuzzy words like "high impact". She also says don't build a checklist that guarantees promotion once enough boxes are ticked ([Fournier, 2025](https://www.elidedbranches.com/2025/05/)). Dropbox's framework says the same thing from the other side: it isn't a promotion checklist, and each responsibility gets three or four key behaviours as a guide, not a full list ([Dropbox](https://dropbox.github.io/dbx-career-framework)).

Fournier has one more tip worth stealing. Write long-form prose alongside the table, even if you never publish it. It's how you catch the gaps a grid of bullet points hides.

Nobody has studied how much detail is the right amount, as far as I can find. My rule of thumb: if a manager can't read one level in under five minutes and explain it to someone, it's too long.

### 2. Competencies or skills?

Competencies describe how someone works: the behaviours from section 3. Skills describe what they can do: "writes SQL", "runs a payroll cycle". You'll hear a lot right now about becoming a "skills-based organisation".

Be careful with that one. A lot of the loudest material comes from consultancies and HR software vendors who sell the transformation, and their surveys mostly measure what executives say they believe. A 2026 paper from SIOP, the industrial-organisational psychology society, says adoption has outrun the research, and that it's too early to draw causal conclusions about whether skills-based practices work. It also says they don't require throwing out jobs, and that a hybrid of skills and existing structures is the most promising route ([Curnow and Belwalkar, SIOP](https://www.siop.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Focal-Can-Skills-Based-Practices-Deliver-A-Framework-for-Understanding-and-Implementation-and-A-Call-for-Research-.pdf)).

What I'd do: use competencies to define levels, because levels are about how someone operates. Use skills in development conversations, because that's where "what do you need to learn next" lives. You don't have to pick one for everything.

### 3. One spine or a ladder per function?

A single spine is one set of levels that works across the company, with the same words for scope and autonomy whether you're in finance or engineering. A per-function ladder spells out what a level looks like in that specific job.

Fournier's advice is not to write a ladder for every job. Use a catch-all scale for most roles, and write dedicated ladders only for big populations where it's worth it ([Fournier, 2025](https://www.elidedbranches.com/2025/05/)). SFIA, a skills framework used widely in digital and IT work, is built the same way: seven generic levels defined by autonomy, influence and complexity, sitting across every skill ([SFIA](https://sfia-online.org/en/about-sfia/how-sfia-works)).

The trap with per-function ladders at your size is maintenance. Every ladder you write is one you have to keep current. Section 8 covers what to do with functions too small for their own track.

### 4. Do you need separate IC and management tracks?

A parallel track lets someone keep growing without managing people. Most published tech ladders have one, and Fournier says the management levels should line up with the IC levels once the tracks split.

This is one of the few decisions with real research behind it, and it's a useful reality check. Researchers at MIT spent years studying dual ladders for engineers and scientists. A 1990 working paper describes a literature with a critical tone and a long list of problems. The main one: people link prestige with power, and climbing the technical ladder brings no more people or visible authority, so it can look less important than management ([Katz, Tushman and Allen, 1990](https://core.ac.uk/download/4379977.pdf)). When the same group asked engineers and scientists in ten organisations which path they'd choose, more picked "interesting project work" than either ladder ([MIT course slides](https://live.ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-310-managerial-psychology-laboratory-spring-2003/6b2ac040484296c622ade808251a3ee0_ocwlecture11bmar1703thedualladdersystempart2.pdf)). It's old research from R&D labs, not modern software companies, but the lesson still holds: if your senior IC title comes with no real say in decisions, people will read it as a consolation prize.

Two practitioner ideas sit on top of that. Charity Majors argues that people should be able to swing between engineering and management over a career rather than pick one forever ([Majors, QCon 2022](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/hands-on-coding-managers)). And Will Larson found that some senior IC roles only exist at scale: in fast-growing tech companies, architect-type roles showed up at around one hundred engineers and "right hand" roles at around one thousand ([Larson, StaffEng](https://staffeng.com/guides/staff-archetypes/)).

That last point matters a lot at your size. If you've got 40 engineers, don't write staff and principal levels you can't fill. It promises a path that doesn't exist yet.

### 5. What does a level actually measure?

Older ladders often tied levels to years of experience or lists of tasks. Most published ladders now describe levels by scope and autonomy instead: how big a problem you own, how much direction you need, how far your influence reaches. Dropbox describes scope as area of ownership and level of autonomy ([Dropbox](https://dropbox.github.io/dbx-career-framework/ic4_quality_engineer.html)). SFIA's levels are built on autonomy, influence and complexity ([SFIA](https://sfia-online.org/en/sfia-9/responsibilities)).

I'd go with scope and autonomy. Years-based levels reward time served, and task lists go out of date the moment your tools change.

### 6. How many levels?

Fournier's advice is to err on the side of more early-career levels, because good junior people want to see progress in their first few years ([Fournier, 2025](https://www.elidedbranches.com/2025/05/)). At the top, the Larson point from above applies: fewer levels, and only ones you can genuinely fill.

### An example

This is illustrative, not a real company.

A 200-person software company has 70 engineers, 40 in customer support and success, and a scattering of people in finance, people, marketing and ops. They start out wanting a full ladder for every function, with IC tracks up to principal.

They end up with:

- One company-wide spine of six levels, written in terms of scope and autonomy.
- A dedicated engineering ladder, because 70 people is a big enough population to justify it, with an IC track up to staff and no principal level yet.
- A dedicated support ladder, because 40 people is too many to leave on a generic description.
- Everyone else on the spine, with a short page per function giving examples of what each level looks like there.

That's three ladders to keep current, plus a short page of examples per function, instead of eight full ladders. They add a principal level when they have someone doing that work, not before.

**What would change my mind:** good evidence that per-function ladders at companies this size lead to better promotion decisions or retention than a shared spine. I haven't found any either way.

## 5. Building it: inputs, drafting, testing, calibration

*Section last reviewed 6 November 2026.*

Building a ladder is mostly about getting the right people involved in the right order. The writing is the easy bit. Whoever you are in this, whether you're in HR, leading a function, managing a team or about to be levelled by the thing, you've got a part to play, and it goes better when everyone knows which part is theirs.

### Who does what

| Who | What they're best placed to do | What goes wrong without them |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HR, People or L&D | Run the process, keep it consistent across functions, own the bias checks, keep the thing maintained | Every function invents its own ladder, nobody checks outcomes, and it rots within a year |
| Function leaders and senior practitioners | Say what good actually looks like in their work | The ladder describes the job in words the people doing it don't recognise |
| Managers | Test it against real people and real decisions | It reads well and falls apart the first time someone uses it to place a person |
| The people being levelled | Read drafts and say where they're unclear, unfair or out of touch | It feels done to them, not with them, and they trust it less |

Two things back this up. Fournier's advice from building her own ladder was that defining expertise in a function belongs with the people in that function ([Fournier, 2025](https://www.elidedbranches.com/2025/05/)). That doesn't mean HR steps back. It means HR runs the room and the function fills in the content.

The second is research. A meta-analysis of 183 studies on fairness at work found that how a decision gets made shapes how fair it feels, separately from the outcome itself ([Colquitt et al., 2001](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/dahe7472/colquitt%202001.pdf)). Giving people a voice in the process is one of the classic ways to make it feel fairer ([Colquitt, 2001](https://typeset.io/pdf/on-the-dimensionality-of-organizational-justice-a-construct-2f8fguv875.pdf)). So asking the people being levelled to read drafts isn't just polite. It's part of what makes the result land.

### Inputs: what to start from

Don't start from a blank page, and don't start from someone else's finished ladder either. Start from three things side by side:

- **A published framework or two.** Dropbox's engineering framework is open source under Apache 2.0 ([GitHub](https://github.com/dropbox/dbx-career-framework)). SFIA has seven generic levels you can borrow the shape of ([SFIA](https://sfia-online.org/en/about-sfia/how-sfia-works)). The UK government's digital and data framework covers product, design and delivery roles as well as engineering ([GOV.UK](https://ddat-capability-framework.service.gov.uk)). Check each one's licence before you copy wording, because they're not all the same.
- **What you actually reward now.** The last handful of promotions, and what got those people there. This is your reality check against the published stuff.
- **The values-to-behaviours work from section 3.** If you skipped it, this is where you'll feel it.

### Drafting

I'd draft in this order. It's my own approach, not a tested method.

1. **Write the spine first.** One sentence per level on scope and autonomy, before any function-specific detail.
2. **Start in the middle.** Describe the level most of your people are at, then work up and down from it. The middle is where you have the most real examples to draw on.
3. **Write a paragraph of prose per level before you make the table.** Fournier recommends this as a way of catching gaps that bullet points hide ([Fournier, 2025](https://www.elidedbranches.com/2025/05/)). You don't have to publish it.
4. **Then make the table.** Three or four behaviours per area per level, not ten.

### Testing the writing

Before anyone gets levelled against it, run these. None of them need a budget.

- **The two-manager test.** Same as section 2. Two managers place the same anonymised person separately. If they're more than a level apart, the writing isn't doing its job yet.
- **The stranger test.** Give one level to someone from a different function and ask them to explain it back. If they can't, it's full of jargon or assumptions.
- **The wording check.** Research on job adverts found that masculine-coded words like "competitive" and "dominant" made jobs less appealing to women, by lowering their sense they'd belong, not their sense they had the skills ([Gaucher, Friesen and Kay, 2011](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/779753)). That was about job ads, not ladders, so I'm applying it by analogy. But a ladder that describes senior people as "aggressive" and "relentless" is telling some readers they're not who you had in mind. There's a free online checker built on the word lists from that paper, described in [this University of Oregon guide](https://provost.uoregon.edu/files/gender_decoder_final.pdf). Treat it as a prompt to look again, not a verdict.
- **The availability check.** Look for anything that really rewards being around rather than being good: "always available", "goes above and beyond", "stays late to get it done". Section 9 explains why this one matters so much.

### Calibration

Calibration means getting the people who'll use the ladder to apply it the same way. There are two very different things people mean by it, and the evidence on them is very different too.

**Calibration as training.** Managers practise placing example cases against the ladder, compare their answers, and talk through where and why they differed. In the research this is called frame-of-reference training, and a meta-analysis found it reliably improves rating accuracy ([Roch et al., 2012](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_1040993122)). This is the strongest evidence in this section, and it's cheap to do.

**Calibration as a committee.** A group reviews and adjusts the levels or ratings managers have proposed. A three-year study at a large multinational found committees adjusted about a quarter of ratings, mostly downwards. That made ratings more consistent across managers and cut leniency, but it also squashed ratings towards the middle, which made it harder to tell people apart ([Demeré, Sedatole and Woods, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2017.3025)). A 2025 study of 737 employees and 114 managers found managers were strategic about what they shared in the room. The ones who found it easier to defend their ratings got fewer adjustments and ended up with higher ratings for their people, even after accounting for performance ([Bol, Braga De Aguiar and Lill, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/hrm.22302)).

Both studies are about performance ratings, not levelling. I think the lessons carry over, but that's my judgement.

What I'd take from them:

- Do the training version first, before anyone's real level is on the table.
- If you run a committee, ask for evidence of work, not adjectives. "Led the migration across three teams" beats "really strong".
- Whoever runs the room, usually HR, should keep track of whose people get adjusted and whose don't. If the confident speakers' teams never move, that's the bias from the second study showing up.
- Check the outcomes by group afterwards. Section 9 covers how.

### An example

This is illustrative, not a real company.

The 200-person company from section 4 is building its support ladder. The People team runs it. The head of support and two senior agents write the first draft, and three support managers test it.

On the two-manager test, they land a level apart on two of the five people they try. Both times it's the same level, mid, where the wording says "handles complex tickets". One manager reads complex as technically hard. The other reads it as emotionally difficult customers. They rewrite it to say both, with an example of each.

The availability check catches "always picks up the queue when it's busy". It sounds like teamwork, but in practice it rewards the people on the late shift, who are mostly the ones without caring responsibilities. They change it to "spots when the queue is in trouble and helps organise a response", which anyone can do on any shift.

Then they run a training calibration with eight past cases before anyone is levelled for real. It takes an afternoon.

**What would change my mind:** evidence that calibration committees used for levelling, rather than performance ratings, behave differently from the ones studied. I haven't found any.

## 6. Adoption: the part everyone skips

*Section last reviewed 6 November 2026.*

Most ladders don't fail on the page. They fail in the months after launch, when it turns out nobody changed how they have conversations or make decisions. This is the longest section because it's where the most time gets wasted.

The good news is that this is better studied than most people realise. It just isn't studied under the name "career ladders". Researchers call it HR implementation: the gap between what a policy says and what managers actually do with it. That research is clear on one thing.

### Your managers on the ground make or break this

However good the ladder is, whether it works comes down to the managers using it day to day. Studies of how HR practices land in real organisations keep finding the same thing: line managers are where a policy either comes to life or quietly fizzles out, and they're a major reason for the gap between what was designed and what people experience ([Purcell and Hutchinson, 2007](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/output/1030432/front-line-managers-as-agents-in-the-hrm-performance-causal-chain-theory-analysis-and-evidence)).

A useful way to break this down is ability, motivation and opportunity. Can the manager do it, do they want to, and does their job give them room to? A survey of 174 managers and over a thousand of their team members found ability was the strongest predictor of how well managers carried out HR practices, and that opportunity made ability count for more ([Bos-Nehles, van Riemsdijk and Looise, 2013](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/employee-perceptions-of-line-management-performance-applying-the-/)).

A study comparing effective and ineffective rollouts across different firms found HR teams made the difference by putting HR people out in the business, framing the practice in ways managers cared about, involving managers in building it, and getting visible backing from the top ([Trullen et al., 2016](https://e-archivo.uc3m.es/handle/10016/34581)).

Here's what that looks like for a ladder:

|  | What it means for a ladder | What helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ability | The manager knows how to use the ladder in a real conversation without it turning into a verdict | Practice, not a slide deck. Section 5's calibration training is a good start |
| Motivation | The manager sees it as something that makes their job easier, not HR admin | Involve them in building it. Show them it answers the "how do I get promoted?" question they're already being asked |
| Opportunity | The manager actually has time, and the ladder is part of decisions they make | Build it into 1:1 templates and promotion decisions so it has somewhere to live |

### Using the ladder in a conversation

This is the bit managers are most nervous about, and they're right to be careful.

The biggest review of feedback research found that feedback improved performance on average, but in over a third of cases it made performance worse. The more feedback pulled attention towards the person and away from the task, the worse it tended to go ([Kluger and DeNisi, 1996](https://pluto.huji.ac.il/%7Emskluger/files/Download/Kluger%20&%20%20DeNisi%20abstract.doc)). The CIPD's own evidence review of feedback says much the same: it can have big effects, but it can also do damage if it's done badly ([CIPD, 2022](https://www.cipd.org/uk/knowledge/evidence-reviews/performance-feedback)).

A ladder makes this risk sharper, because a level is easy to hear as a verdict on who you are. So the practical rule is to talk about the work, using the ladder to describe what the next step looks like, not to grade the person.

Some examples of the difference. These aren't a script, just illustrations.

| Sounds like a verdict | Sounds like a conversation about the work |
| --- | --- |
| "You're not operating at a senior level yet." | "On the migration, you set the plan for your own team really well. At senior, I'd expect you to bring the two other teams along too. What would that have looked like?" |
| "You need to work on your influence." | "In the pricing meeting, your analysis was right and it still didn't land. Want to talk through what might have got it over the line?" |
| "You ticked most of the boxes, but not all." | "Here's the one area where I haven't seen enough yet, and here's a project coming up where you could show it." |
| "Everyone else at your level is doing more." | "Here's what the level asks for, and here's where I think you are against it. Where do you see yourself?" |

The last row matters most. Comparing someone to their peers points straight at the self, which is exactly the kind of feedback the research flags as risky.

### The checklist trap

The moment a ladder becomes a list of boxes to tick for promotion, people start working to the boxes. Ordóñez and colleagues argued that specific goals bring predictable side effects, including people narrowing their focus and neglecting anything that isn't measured ([Ordóñez et al., 2009](https://repository.upenn.edu/mgmt_papers/325)). That paper started a proper academic argument. Locke and Latham, who built much of the goal-setting research, replied that the critique leaned on anecdotes ([Locke and Latham, 2009](https://econbiz.de/Record/has-goal-setting-gone-wild-or-have-its-attackers-abandoned-good-scholarship-locke-edwin/10009893943)), and the original authors replied to that ([Ordóñez et al., 2009](https://repository.upenn.edu/mgmt_papers/318)). So the research on this is genuinely contested, and ladders aren't goals in the strict sense anyway.

Where there's no contest is in practice. Dropbox says outright that its framework isn't a promotion checklist ([Dropbox](https://dropbox.github.io/dbx-career-framework)), and Fournier warns against ladders that guarantee promotion once enough boxes are ticked ([Fournier, 2025](https://www.elidedbranches.com/2025/05/)).

Signs you've fallen into it, as I see them:

- People ask "which boxes am I missing?" instead of "what should I work on?"
- Promotion cases read like a list of matched bullet points, with no story about the work.
- Useful work that isn't on the ladder stops getting done. Section 9 picks this up, because the work that disappears first is usually the less visible kind.

### Forcing functions: giving the ladder somewhere to live

A forcing function is anything that makes the ladder part of a decision it should already be part of. Without one, the ladder lives in a wiki and gets opened twice a year.

There's some evidence this kind of structure matters. When one large company made managers accountable for explaining their pay decisions, and made the process more transparent, the unexplained pay gaps by gender, race and nationality shrank ([Castilla, 2015](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/374640)). The nursing clinical-ladder research is weaker, mostly programme evaluations, but one review of 29 studies listed mandatory participation among its recommendations, and found organisational culture mattered more than anything else ([Moore, Meucci and McGrath, 2019](https://scholars.uthscsa.edu/en/publications/attributes-of-a-successful-clinical-ladder-program-for-nurses-an-/)).

The specific forcing functions below are my suggestions, not tested methods:

- **Promotion cases reference the ladder.** Every case says which level it's arguing for and gives examples of the work against it.
- **The ladder appears in the 1:1 template.** Not every week. Once a quarter is plenty.
- **New managers get the ladder in their first month.** Along with a practice conversation, not just a link.
- **Leaders use it out loud.** When the leadership team talks about a promotion, they use the ladder's words. People notice when they don't.

Forcing functions can turn into box-ticking of their own. If managers are filling in a ladder field in a template without having the conversation, you've moved the checklist, not removed it.

### Champions

A lot of rollout advice says to find champions: respected people who'll get behind the ladder and help others use it.

The evidence here is honestly mixed. A review of 199 studies of champions in healthcare found the word was used inconsistently, that champions' success varied, and that very few studies had tested them properly ([Miech et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5960847)). The training research is a bit more encouraging. A meta-analysis of 89 studies found a supportive work environment helps people actually use what they learn, especially for open-ended skills like leadership ([Blume et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206309352880)). A 2020 meta-analysis found support from peers explained more of that than support from supervisors or the organisation ([Hughes et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720819845988)).

So my read is this. Don't bet the rollout on a few enthusiastic champions. Do give managers a peer they can practise with and ask "how would you handle this?" That's closer to what the research supports.

### A rollout order

This is the sequence I'd use. It's my view, built from the research above, not a tested programme.

1. **Leadership agrees to use it themselves.** If the people making promotion decisions won't use the ladder's words, stop here and go back to section 2's political problem.
2. **Managers get it before anyone else.** With a practice session, using past cases.
3. **Managers each have one practice conversation.** With a peer, before a real one.
4. **Everyone else gets it, from their manager.** Not from an all-hands announcement or an email from HR. The manager's conversation is the launch.
5. **The forcing functions switch on.** Promotion cases and the quarterly 1:1 change at the same time.
6. **Check at three months.** Rerun the last-looked question from section 2. If most people still say "never", something in steps 1 to 5 didn't happen.

### An example

This is illustrative, not a real company.

Back to the engineering manager from sections 1 and 2. She found her problem was adoption, not design, so she doesn't rewrite the ladder.

She asks the CTO to use the ladder's language in the next promotion discussion, and he agrees. She runs a 90-minute session with the other managers on three anonymised past promotions. She adds one question to the quarterly 1:1 template: "Which part of the next level are we working on, and what's the project that'll show it?" New promotion cases now have to name the level and give two examples against it.

Three months later, she asks the same five people when they last looked at the ladder. Three say "in my last 1:1". It's not perfect, but it's moved, and she didn't write a single new word of ladder.

### How sure is this?

The parts about managers and feedback are well supported. The specific forcing functions and the rollout order are mine, built on that research but not tested as a package. Nobody I can find has run a proper study of ladder rollouts in companies this size.

**What would change my mind:** a well-run comparison showing ladders launched through an all-hands and a document did as well as ladders launched through managers. I'd be surprised, but I'd want to know.

## 7. Living with it: cadence, ownership, versioning

*Section last reviewed 6 November 2026.*

A ladder that never changes slowly stops describing the work. One that changes every few weeks is hard to trust, because nobody knows which version they're being measured against. The aim is a steady rhythm that everyone can see.

### How the big public frameworks do it

The long-running public frameworks have all settled on something similar, and it's worth borrowing.

- **SFIA** runs a three-year cycle for major versions. Anyone can submit a change request at any time, proposed changes go out as a public beta before release, and old versions stay available ([SFIA](https://sfia-online.org/en/sfia-10)). Their own consultation also flags that frequent changes cost smaller teams a lot of effort, and suggests smaller updates between big ones ([SFIA governance](https://sfia-online.org/en/sfia-10/topics-identified-from-sfia-10-consultation-workshops/framework-governance-how-sfia-evolves)).
- **The UK government's digital and data framework** publishes a dated list of every update on its front page, and has a public route to propose a change ([GOV.UK](https://ddat-capability-framework.service.gov.uk/)).
- **The CIPD Profession Map** has a set twice-yearly review ([CIPD](https://prod.cipd.org/ae/the-people-profession/the-profession-map/faqs/updates)).

The common thread: small fixes happen often, big changes happen rarely and in the open, there's a visible record of what changed, and anyone can suggest something.

### A rhythm for your size

This is what I'd suggest for a company of 50 to 500. Adjust it to how fast you're changing.

| How often | What happens | Who's involved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whenever it's needed | Typos, unclear wording, broken links. Nobody's level changes | Whoever owns the ladder |
| Once a year | Each function leader reads their part against real work and the year's calibration notes. Wording gets tightened | Function leaders, with the owner running it |
| Every two to three years, or after a big change in the company | A proper look at the structure: levels added or removed, tracks split or merged | Leadership signs it off, managers and employees see drafts first |

Some things should trigger a review whatever the calendar says:

- A function grows big enough to need its own ladder, or shrinks so it doesn't.
- The same wording keeps causing arguments in calibration.
- The checks in section 9 show outcomes drifting apart for one group.
- Managers have quietly stopped using it. Rerun the last-looked question from section 2 once a year to find out.

### Who owns it

One named owner, not "HR" in general. In most companies your size that's someone in the People team, because they can see across every function. Owning it means running the reviews, keeping the changelog and making sure suggestions get an answer. It doesn't mean writing every word.

The content still belongs with the functions, as in section 5. Bigger structural changes need leadership to agree, because they change what people are working towards.

### Versions and the changelog

Keep a version number and a dated changelog, the same way this playbook does. A simple rule:

- **Big number** when a level is added, removed or changes meaning. These can change someone's level, so they need notice and an explanation.
- **Small number** when wording changes but nobody's level should move.
- **Smallest number** for fixes.

Each changelog line says what changed, why, and who it affects. Two more habits that save a lot of grief later:

- **Keep old versions available**, the way SFIA does. If someone was promoted against last year's ladder, you need to be able to see what last year's ladder said.
- **Note the version on every promotion case.** It's one line, and it ends a whole category of argument.

### Taking suggestions

Give people one place to suggest changes, and tell them what happens next. SFIA shows every change request with a status, such as pending, accepted or deferred ([SFIA change requests](https://sfia-online.org/en/sfia-9/change-requests)). You don't need a website for that. A shared tracker that everyone can see does the job, as long as nothing sits there unanswered for months.

### An example

This is illustrative, not a real company.

It's a year since the 200-person company from sections 4 and 5 launched its ladders. The People partner who owns them sends each function leader their section and the calibration notes from the year.

Support comes back with two wording fixes and one bigger point. They've started a small onboarding team, and the ladder doesn't describe that work at all. Engineering hasn't changed anything, but three calibration arguments that year were about the same line on "technical leadership", so they rewrite it.

The result is version 1.1. No levels move, the changelog has five lines, and every promotion case from now on says it was assessed against 1.1. The onboarding question goes on the list for the next structural review rather than being rushed in.

**What would change my mind:** evidence that companies this size do better with continuous small edits and no fixed cycle. I can see the appeal, but I haven't found anything showing it works better.

## 8. Edge cases

*Section last reviewed 6 November 2026.*

Every ladder hits the same handful of awkward situations. None of them have a perfect answer, but they all go better when you've thought about them before someone's sitting across from you asking.

### Functions too small for their own track

Your three-person finance team doesn't need its own ladder. Neither does a one-person legal function or a two-person office team. Writing one for each is a lot of work to maintain for very few people.

What tends to work better is the shared spine from section 4, plus a short page of examples for that function: what scope and autonomy look like at each level in their actual work. Fournier suggests the same, a catch-all scale for most roles and dedicated ladders only where the population is big enough ([Fournier, 2025](https://www.elidedbranches.com/2025/05/)). If the function has a professional body, its framework can help fill in the detail. The CIPD Profession Map does that for people roles, for example ([CIPD](https://cipd.org/en/the-people-profession/the-profession-map/explore-the-profession-map)).

The harder bit is honesty about where the path ends. In a three-person team, there may be no next level to step into without someone leaving. Fournier's advice here is to be honest about each job family's shape, rather than implying everyone can climb to the top ([Fournier, 2025](https://www.elidedbranches.com/2025/05/)). I'd say that out loud in the career conversation. It's kinder than letting someone find out after three years of waiting.

### Hybrid roles

Some people don't fit neatly in one function: the support engineer who writes code, the ops person who also runs recruitment. My suggestion is to level them on the spine by scope and autonomy, then use the closest function's examples as a guide, not a rulebook. Write down which one you used and why, so the next manager doesn't have to guess.

### First rollout or revamp?

These feel similar from the outside, but they land very differently.

**A first rollout** is people being given a level for the first time. Most people end up where they expected. Some end up higher, because they've been doing more than their title said for a while. Section 9 covers why that group is so often the same kinds of people.

**A revamp** is people moving from one ladder to another, and they will compare. Anyone whose new level looks lower than their old one will experience it as a demotion, even if the pay and the work stay exactly the same.

That matters, because demotion lands hard. Interviews with people who'd been involuntarily demoted found turnover intentions and lower motivation and commitment, tied to whether the process felt fair. They also found stigma, a sense of lost status and a threat to people's sense of who they are ([Hennekam and Ananthram, 2020](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:hal:journl:hal-03232764)). Job titles are a big part of that identity. Research has described them as identity badges that anchor how people see themselves at work ([Grant, Berg and Cable, 2014](https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2012.0338)).

So for a revamp, as I see it:

- **Map everyone before anyone hears anything.** Find every case where someone's level or title would look lower, and decide what you're doing about each one first.
- **Tell those people individually, first.** Not in a group announcement, and not by them spotting it in a spreadsheet.
- **Explain why the mapping works the way it does.** The section 5 research on fair process applies here too. People take a hard outcome better when they understand how it was reached and had a say.
- **Give people a way to question it.** A named person and a clear route, not "talk to your manager" if the manager made the call.

### Grandfathering

Grandfathering means letting someone keep a level or title that the new ladder wouldn't give them. There are a few ways to handle it, and none are perfect.

| Option | What it looks like | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Keep the title, be honest about the gap | They keep their current title. Their manager is clear about what the new ladder expects and agrees a plan to close the gap | Two people doing the same work can have different titles for a while, and others will notice |
| Map them honestly, protect what matters to them | They move to the level the ladder gives them, with any protections the company decides on | It feels like a demotion, however well it's handled |
| Re-level upwards | The new ladder shows they're under-titled, so they move up | It costs money and may raise questions about how it happened. Section 9 says why it's worth it anyway |

What I'd avoid is grandfathering forever without saying so. It quietly creates two ladders, the written one and the one some people are on. If you do grandfather, put a date on it, agree a plan, and write it down.

One more check. Look at who ends up grandfathered and who ends up re-mapped. If the people keeping generous titles are mostly one group, and the people being moved down are mostly another, that's the kind of pattern section 9 is there to catch.

Any protection on pay belongs in the pay volume, not this one. And changes to titles in someone's contract are worth checking locally before you make them.

### An example

This is illustrative, not a real company.

A 350-person company is replacing a five-year-old ladder that grew a new title every time someone asked. Mapping everyone onto the new spine shows 14 people whose title would look lower, and 9 whose level would go up.

The People team and the relevant managers go through all 23 before anything is announced. For the 14, each manager has a one-to-one first. Most keep their current title for a year, with a written plan for what the new level asks of them. Two decide they'd rather take the new title straight away, because it describes their work better. For the 9, they move up at launch. When the People team looks at who's in which group, the 9 being moved up are mostly women, mostly in support and operations, which tells them something about how the old titles were handed out. Section 9 picks up from there.

**What would change my mind:** evidence that a revamp announced all at once, with individual conversations afterwards, landed as well as one where the affected people heard first. I haven't seen any, and the demotion research points the other way.

## 9. Bias checks and anti-patterns

*Section last reviewed 6 November 2026.*

This section isn't an appendix. Levelling is one of the moments where unfairness that's built up over years either gets noticed or gets written down and made official. When you map people to levels, you often find that some of them have been doing more than their title said for a long time, and they tend to be the same kinds of people. A ladder that doesn't check for that gives the pattern a stamp of approval.

The good news is that this is the best-evidenced part of the whole playbook, so there's a lot to work with.

### What the research shows

**Calling yourself a meritocracy can make things worse.** In three experiments with 445 people who had management experience, participants gave a man a bigger bonus than an equally performing woman when the company was described as meritocratic ([Castilla and Benard, 2010](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/367841)). The researchers called it the paradox of meritocracy. If your launch messaging leans hard on "this makes us fair now", it's worth knowing that.

**"Potential" is where a lot of bias hides.** In a study of nearly 30,000 management-track employees at a large retail chain, women got lower ratings for potential despite getting higher performance ratings, and that gap explained around half of the gender gap in promotions. Women then went on to outperform their male colleagues, both on average and among those close to promotion ([Benson, Li and Shue, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20220831)). A lab experiment on disability found something similar: raters judged past performance fairly, but stereotypes about which disabilities suit which jobs still shaped what they expected someone to do in future ([Colella and Varma, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1021362019948)). Both point the same way. Criteria about what someone might do are riskier than criteria about what they've done.

**Some people get handed the work that doesn't get promoted.** Women were more likely than men to volunteer, be asked, and say yes to tasks with low promotability, like committee work or writing up reports. The reason was other people's expectation that women would say yes, not a difference in preference ([Babcock et al., 2017](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/1236700-gender-differences-in-accepting-and-receiving-requests-for-tasks-with-low-promotability)). In tech, Tanya Reilly gave this a name: glue work, the less glamorous work a team needs to succeed, which ladders often don't reward ([Reilly, Being Glue](https://noidea.dog/glue); there's also a [LeadDev video of the talk](https://leaddev.com/community/tanya-reilly)). Her challenge to managers is a good one: if your ladder doesn't ask senior people for those skills, how are you expecting that work to get done?

**Rewarding availability penalises carers.** Researchers call it flexibility stigma: people who use flexible working, or are assumed to need it, get seen as less committed. Many avoid using flexible policies because they fear lower evaluations and fewer promotions ([APA summary of the research](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2013/12/stigma); [Williams, Blair-Loy and Berdahl, 2013](https://scienceopen.com/document/vid/a75563ea-00bf-4a19-9073-fac49c5d650e)). Ladder lines like "always available" or "goes the extra mile out of hours" write that stigma straight into the criteria.

**Under-levelling often starts at hire.** An experiment with hiring managers found overqualified women and sufficiently qualified men tended to be hired into the same jobs and ranks. Managers worried overqualified men were a flight risk, but explained away the same overqualification in women ([Campbell and Hahl, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2021.1550)). If that's happened at your company, your first mapping exercise is where it shows up.

**Race effects are real, and the evidence is thinner than it should be.** An early meta-analysis of 74 studies found race effects in performance ratings were more likely in real workplaces where Black employees made up a small share of the workforce ([Kraiger and Ford, 1985](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/facpubs/11893)). That's old research, but it describes a situation a lot of 50 to 500 person companies are in. Castilla's field study, below, found gaps by race and nationality as well as gender.

**Disabled employees get paid less for comparable work.** A meta-analysis of 50 studies covering more than 5.7 million workers found employees with disabilities performed at broadly comparable levels to their colleagues, earned significantly less, and reported more unmet accommodation needs ([Zhou, Upadhyay and Viswesvaran, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvb.2025.104205)).

### What actually helps

The clearest evidence for a fix comes from Castilla's study of almost 9,000 employees at one large company. Before the change, women, ethnic minorities and non-US-born employees got smaller rewards than white US-born men with the same performance scores, in the same jobs, under the same managers. After the company brought in accountability and transparency to its decisions, that gap shrank ([Castilla, 2015](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/374640)).

For a ladder, as I see it, that means:

- **Decisions get explained, in writing, against the ladder.** Not "we felt she wasn't ready", but which part of the level and what evidence.
- **Someone looks at the outcomes, by group, every cycle.** And has the standing to ask questions when something looks off.
- **The criteria are about what people have done, not who they seem to be.** Swap "has leadership presence" for what leading actually looked like in the work.

### Checks before you launch

Run the ladder through these. They're my checks, built from the research above.

1. **The potential check.** Find every line that's about future promise rather than past work. Rewrite it or cut it.
2. **The availability check.** From section 5. Anything rewarding being around rather than being good.
3. **The glue check.** Does the senior level ask for the coordination, mentoring and unblocking work that keeps a team going? If not, who's doing it now, and will this ladder count it?
4. **The wording check.** From section 5. Coded words that tell some readers this isn't for them.
5. **The authorship check.** Who wrote and reviewed it? If it's all one kind of person, get more eyes on it before anyone's levelled against it.

### Checks after every mapping and every cycle

1. **Compare outcomes by group.** Who landed where at mapping, who got promoted, how long people sit at each level, and whose levels calibration moved.
2. **Look at grandfathering.** From section 8. Who kept a generous title, and who got moved down?
3. **Look at the under-titled.** Who moved up at mapping? If it's mostly one group, that tells you something about how titles were handed out before.
4. **Read the promotion cases side by side.** Are some people's cases described in specifics and others' in vague praise? Same evidence, different words is often where bias shows.

At 50 to 500 people, the numbers in each group will often be too small for statistics to mean much. I'd still look, but treat it as a reason to read the individual cases closely, not as proof either way. How you collect and store data on things like ethnicity or disability is worth checking locally before you start.

### Anti-patterns

These are the patterns that come up again and again across this playbook. The evidence for each is in the section it links to.

| Anti-pattern | Why it hurts | Where it's covered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adding detail to fix an adoption problem | The ladder gets longer and less used | Section 2 |
| Writing values into the ladder that leaders don't live | It makes the gap between words and actions official | Section 3 |
| Copying a big-tech ladder wholesale | It assumes a size and structure you don't have | Section 4 |
| Senior levels nobody can reach | It promises a path that doesn't exist | Section 4 |
| The checklist ladder | People work to the boxes and stop doing what isn't listed | Section 6 |
| Launching by announcement | Managers never learn to use it, so nobody does | Section 6 |
| Silent demotion in a revamp | People find out the hard way and trust drops | Section 8 |
| Criteria about potential or availability | They bring in bias the research has already found | This section |
| Never checking outcomes by group | Old unfairness gets an official stamp | This section |
| "This makes us a meritocracy" | The research suggests it can make bias worse | This section |

### What I haven't covered well yet

Most of the strong evidence here is about gender, and most of it comes from the US. The research on race in levelling specifically is thinner and older than it should be. The disability research is mostly about pay and hiring rather than levels. I haven't yet looked properly for research on how levelling affects LGBTQ+ people, neurodivergent people, or people working across countries and cultures. Those are top of the list for the next version. If you know of good work on any of them, please send it.

### An example

This is illustrative, not a real company.

Back to the 350-person company from section 8. The 9 people moving up are mostly women in support and operations, so the People team digs further.

They find the old ladder had a line at senior level, "recognised as a go-to expert across the company", that was almost always met by people in visible, client-facing engineering roles. The support leads doing the same scope of work weren't seen that way, because their work happened in tickets and internal channels. They also find three of the 9 had been told in the past that they "weren't quite ready" without any reasons written down.

The new ladder already replaces that line with what the work involves: owning a problem area across teams and making sure it gets solved. They add one more thing. From now on, any "not yet" has to say which part of the level and what evidence would change it, written down and shared with the person.

**What would change my mind:** good evidence that transparent, written decisions against clear criteria didn't reduce gaps, or made them worse. Castilla's study is one company, and I'd like to see it repeated.

## 10. Sources, confidence labels and changelog

*Section last reviewed 6 November 2026.*

Everything this playbook relies on is listed here, grouped by what kind of source it is, so you can judge it for yourself.

### How to read the sources

- **Research** is peer-reviewed or academic work. Where a finding is old, small, from one company or from a lab, the section that uses it says so.
- **Published frameworks** are real frameworks organisations have put out for others to use. They show what's done, not what works.
- **Practitioner writing** is experienced people sharing what they've learned. It's useful, but it's not research.
- **Professional bodies** set standards for a profession. Worth reading, not neutral on everything.

Where a source's author or publisher sells the thing it recommends, the section that uses it says so.
