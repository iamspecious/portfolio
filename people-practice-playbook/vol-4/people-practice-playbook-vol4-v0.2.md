## 1. Who this is for, and what it isn't

*Section last reviewed 12 November 2026.*

> **If you need help right now:** call or text **9-8-8** (Canada, 24/7) for the [Suicide Crisis Helpline](https://988.ca/). If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call **9-1-1** or your local emergency number (9-1-1 isn't available everywhere in Canada; see section 8). Deaf and hard-of-hearing people can reach 9-8-8 through Canada VRS, TTY (dial 7-1-1) or IP Relay. **Outside Canada:** section 8 lists a few countries, and [Find A Helpline](https://findahelpline.com/) covers many more.

A safety plan is something you put together *before* you need it, so that when something goes wrong you aren't trying to think clearly in the middle of a crisis. Most people know safety plans from suicide prevention or from leaving an abusive relationship. They help in many other situations too.

This volume is for two groups:

- **Anyone who wants to make a plan,** for themselves or with someone they support. Section 4 is a checklist and section 9 is a blank plan you can copy.
- **HR and People teams, managers and business owners.** You may not build anyone's safety plan, but you may be one of the people someone tells. Section 7 covers what to do and what not to, and how to scale it whether you're a five-person business with no HR or a multi-country employer.

It started with a break-room conversation about supports for 2SLGBTQI+ staff, where few people knew what a safety plan was. Section 6 covers coming out, because that's where the question came from.

**The short version:**

- **A safety plan is a short, personal, practical document:** who to contact, where to go, what to take, and how to signal for help.
- **Anyone can need one.** Abuse, harassment, stalking and crisis are not limited by gender, sexual orientation or stage of life.
- **Some safety steps can raise the risk.** Changing passwords or switching off location sharing can alert the person you're worried about. Section 4 explains how to plan those steps safely.
- **HR's job is to listen, believe and connect,** not to investigate or to take over. Any plan is made with the person's consent and at their direction.
- **If you're worried someone is thinking about suicide, ask them directly.** Crisis services recommend it, and research hasn't found that asking makes things worse (section 3).

**What it isn't.** It isn't legal advice, and it isn't a substitute for a crisis worker, shelter worker, counsellor or the police. Where the law comes into it (reporting duties, protected leave, criminal harassment), the rules vary by province and territory, so check what applies to you with someone qualified. That's true in every country. The fullest resource list is for Canada, where this volume started, but the checklist, the evidence and the HR guidance work anywhere. Section 8 also lists services in the US, the UK, Ireland, Germany and Australia, and a directory for other countries.

**About the research.** This volume was researched and drafted with AI, and every source was opened and checked before it went in. Phone numbers and hours were checked on each service's own website on 12 November 2026, except where section 8 says otherwise. Services change, so check before you rely on one.

## 2. What a safety plan is

*Section last reviewed 12 November 2026.*

A safety plan is a personalised, practical document that sets out the steps you can take to keep yourself safe when there's a risk to your physical, emotional or psychological wellbeing. It's a set of actions, contacts, resources and strategies you put together in advance.

That definition is close to how [Shelter Safe](https://sheltersafe.ca/about/safety-planning/), run by Women's Shelters Canada, describes a safety plan for domestic violence: "a personalized, practical plan for ways to stay safe while in a relationship, planning to leave, or even after you leave." Shelter Safe also says: "A safety plan isn't an escape plan." It covers staying, leaving and afterwards.

Different fields use different versions:

- **Suicide prevention.** Canada's 9-8-8 helpline uses the Stanley-Brown Safety Plan, and offers a [free template](https://988.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/9-8-8-Safety-Plan-Template-English.pdf) with six steps: your warning signs; things you can do on your own to take your mind off things; people and places that give you a distraction; people you can ask for help; professionals and services you can contact; and making your surroundings safer.
- **Abuse and violence.** Shelter workers help people plan around the danger they face. Shelter Safe says each shelter's process differs but they all cover identifying danger, assessing risk and planning for safety, and that staff will encourage you to rehearse the plan: "This way it's easier to implement if you ever need to use it."
- **Technology abuse.** [Tech Safety Canada](https://techsafety.ca/resources/toolkits/tech-safety-planning-toolkit), also run by Women's Shelters Canada, has a toolkit for planning around someone misusing phones, accounts or devices to monitor, harass or stalk.
- **At work.** The Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety (CCOHS) recommends workplaces [help create individual personal and workplace safety plans](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/psychosocial/violence_domestic.html) when intimate partner or family violence follows someone to work.

The checklist in section 4 combines these into one general-purpose plan. If one of the situations above is yours, use the specialist version too. It goes into more detail, and the service behind it can help you.

## 3. What the evidence says, and where it runs out

*Section last reviewed 12 November 2026.*

### Suicide prevention: the strongest evidence

A 2021 meta-analysis in the *British Journal of Psychiatry* pooled six studies of brief safety planning interventions, covering 3,536 adults ([Nuij and colleagues, 2021](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/238354/), free copy). People who made a safety plan were less likely to go on to suicidal behaviour than people who didn't: a relative risk of 0.57, meaning about 16 people would need to make a plan to prevent one case of suicidal behaviour. The analysis found no significant effect on suicidal *thoughts*.

The authors are clear about the limits. Only six studies qualified. Four were judged at high risk of bias, partly because two weren't randomised. There were signs of publication bias, and after adjusting for it the effect was smaller (a relative risk of 0.71) but still significant. All the participants were adults, so the results can't be assumed to hold for young people. In every study, the plan was made with a clinician.

**What that means here:** there is peer-reviewed evidence that safety planning helps prevent suicidal behaviour, when a clinician helps make the plan. It's less clear how much a plan you make on your own helps, and the evidence doesn't show that it makes difficult thoughts go away. A plan is a tool for getting through a crisis safely.

### Young people: the evidence is thinner

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in *JAMA Pediatrics*, led by researchers at SickKids in Toronto, looked at safety planning used on its own with children and adolescents ([Albaum and colleagues, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2025.1012); [summary from SickKids](https://www.sickkids.ca/en/news/archive/2025/study-investigates-efficacy-of-safety-planning-interventions-for-suicide-prevention-among-children-and-adolescents/)). It found 10 studies with 1,002 adolescents, and five of them could be pooled. Safety planning on its own wasn't significantly linked to fewer suicidal thoughts, suicide attempts, other suicide-related behaviour or return visits to hospital. The authors call the evidence for young people limited, and say most of the studies were small pilots.

**What that means here:** for young people, a safety plan shouldn't be the only support. That isn't a reason not to make one. It's a reason to make sure a young person also has adults and services around them, such as Kids Help Phone (section 8).

### Abuse and violence: some benefit, weak evidence

Shelters, crisis lines and workplace safety bodies all recommend safety planning for abuse and violence (section 2). It's harder to measure what safety planning does here, because it's usually one part of a wider package of support.

- **Advocacy.** A Campbell systematic review pooled 13 trials of advocacy for women experiencing intimate partner abuse. Advocacy there includes support with safety planning, alongside counselling and help reaching other services ([Rivas and colleagues, 2016](https://doi.org/10.4073/csr.2016.2), free to read). The authors found "some benefits": brief advocacy in healthcare settings was linked to less depression, and intensive advocacy for women recruited from shelters was linked to less severe physical abuse at two years, though not at one or three. But most studies were too small, and the quality of the evidence was rated moderate to very low.
- **Online safety planning.** A Canadian trial gave 462 women who had recently experienced partner violence either a tailored, interactive online safety and health tool (iCAN Plan 4 Safety) or a basic, non-tailored version ([Ford-Gilboe and colleagues, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-020-8152-8), free to read). Women in both groups had fewer symptoms of depression and post-traumatic stress over the following year, and rated both tools as safe and helpful. The two groups didn't differ overall, though the tailored version did better for some groups, including women with children at home and women facing more severe violence. My reading: because everyone got a version of the tool, the trial can't show how much of the improvement came from the tool rather than time.

**What that means here:** there's enough evidence to say safety planning, especially with an advocate, can help, and these studies reported little sign of harm. There isn't enough to say how much it helps, or which parts matter most. Most of this research is on women, so it says little about men or gender-diverse people.

### Other situations

For the other situations in section 4 (coming out, workplace bullying, custody disputes, relocating), I found no research testing safety plans directly. Recommending one there is my judgement, not a research finding: the reasons that make planning ahead useful in a crisis apply just as much there.

### Is it safe to ask someone about suicide?

Many people worry that asking will put the idea in someone's head. A 2014 review in *Psychological Medicine* looked at 13 studies of adults and adolescents, in both general and at-risk groups, and none found a statistically significant rise in suicidal thoughts from being asked ([Dazzi, Gribble, Wessely and Fear, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291714001299)). The authors suggest talking about it may even reduce those thoughts. Those studies were in research, school and clinical settings, not workplaces, so they don't tell us how a conversation with a manager goes. But they suggest that asking doesn't cause harm.

Canada's 9-8-8 gives the same advice: if you think someone might be considering suicide, "it's usually better to ask them directly" ([9-8-8](https://988.ca/get-help/talk-to-someone-you-are-worried-about)). Section 7 covers what to do next.

### Why HR should care: who people tell

In a 2014 pan-Canadian survey of 8,429 people by researchers at Western University and the Canadian Labour Congress ([Wathen, MacGregor and MacQuarrie](https://www.uwo.ca/projects/heritage/heritage3/img/survey-report.pdf)), a third (33.6%) of respondents said they had experienced domestic violence. Of those, 81.9% said it hurt their work performance, 38% said it affected their ability to get to work, and 8.5% said they had lost a job because of it. Respondents who were Indigenous, disabled, or other than heterosexual were particularly likely to report it.

Of those who had experienced it, 43.2% had talked about it with someone at work. Those people talked most often to **co-workers (81.6%) and supervisors or managers (44.7%)**. Only 10.7% of those who talked to someone at work talked to HR. Where a workplace had someone designated to handle domestic violence, that person most often helped by developing a safety plan.

Two caveats. The survey was recruited mostly through union networks, so most respondents were unionised (81.4%) and most were women (78.4%). It isn't a random sample of Canadian workers. And it's from 2014.

**What that means for HR:** you're often not the first person someone tells. Managers and co-workers are. So the most useful thing HR can do may be making sure managers know what a safety plan is and where to send people.

## 4. What goes in a plan

*Section last reviewed 12 November 2026.*

Every situation is different, so take what fits and leave the rest. A crisis worker, shelter worker or counsellor can help you tailor it.

### Who safety plans are for

My take: everyone. But they're especially relevant if you are:

- Coming out, and worried about how family, a partner, housemates or your workplace might react
- In, or leaving, an abusive relationship (emotional, physical, sexual, financial or through technology)
- Experiencing stalking or criminal harassment
- Experiencing harassment or bullying at work
- Going through a mental health crisis, or have been through one before
- In a custody dispute
- Relocating for safety reasons
- In any situation where conflict, confrontation or a hard conversation could escalate in a way that threatens your safety

None of these are limited by gender, sexual orientation or circumstance. CCOHS puts it directly: anyone can be a target of violence, "regardless of gender, age, race, culture, religion, sexual orientation, socioeconomic status, or educational background" ([CCOHS](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/psychosocial/violence_domestic.html)).

### Before you change anything: plan the risky steps

Some of the steps below can alert the person you're worried about. Tech Safety Canada warns that changing passwords, location settings or account access "will often alert the other person," who "might become more abusive," and that changes can also erase evidence ([Survivors' Guide to Phones](https://techsafety.ca/resources/toolkits/survivors-guide-to-phones-increasing-privacy-and-responding-to-abuse/); [Location Tracking guide](https://techsafety.ca/resources/toolkits/a-survivors-guide-to-location-tracking/)). Its advice:

- **Document first.** Screenshot and log what's happening before you change anything.
- **Use a safer device** to research and plan: one the person can't get into and hasn't had access to in the past, such as a library computer or a friend's phone ([Tech Safety Planning Toolkit](https://techsafety.ca/resources/toolkits/tech-safety-planning-toolkit)).
- **Decide on timing.** Some people keep a monitored phone or location sharing as it is for a while, so nothing looks different, and make the changes when it's safe to.
- **Talk it through with an advocate.** Shelter workers do this planning every day, whether or not you ever stay in a shelter, and [Shelter Safe](https://sheltersafe.ca/) can connect you. Its network is women's shelters, so if that isn't the right fit for you, victim services and the other routes in section 8 can help.

If none of that applies to you, go ahead with the steps as written.

### People

- [ ] A trusted contact who knows your situation and can be reached at any time
- [ ] A backup contact, in case the first isn't available
- [ ] An out-of-area contact: someone outside your immediate surroundings who can coordinate if needed
- [ ] Professional contacts, if you have them: a therapist, doctor, lawyer, social worker or advocate
- [ ] Crisis lines saved in your phone under names that won't draw attention, if that matters for you (section 8)

### Places

- [ ] A safe place you can go if you need to leave home quickly
- [ ] A backup place
- [ ] Where your nearest shelter, crisis centre, hospital and police station are

### Documents and essentials

- [ ] Copies of important documents kept somewhere safe, away from home: ID, passport, health card, insurance, medical records, legal papers, lease or mortgage, immigration papers
- [ ] Emergency money: even a small amount of cash, or an account that isn't held jointly
- [ ] A packed bag, or a written list of what you'd grab
- [ ] Medications, prescriptions and anything you need daily (glasses, mobility aids, chargers)
- [ ] A plan for children, pets and anyone who depends on you

### Digital safety

Read "Before you change anything" above first.

- [ ] Know who has access to your devices, accounts and location
- [ ] Know how to turn location sharing on and off on your phone
- [ ] A separate email address that isn't linked to shared accounts or devices. Tech Safety Canada notes a second email or secure phone number also lets you [set up two-step verification](https://techsafety.ca/resources/toolkits/tips-for-a-secure-email-account/)
- [ ] Change passwords and PINs if they may have been compromised, at a time that's safe
- [ ] If someone else holds or can get into your phone account, know that they may be able to [see call logs and billing records and turn on location features](https://techsafety.ca/resources/toolkits/survivors-guide-to-phones-increasing-privacy-and-responding-to-abuse/)
- [ ] Use private browsing when researching help. Shelter Safe notes that [clearing *all* your history may itself look suspicious](https://sheltersafe.ca/about/staysafe/) to someone who checks. Private browsing won't hide anything from spyware, so if that's a possibility, use a safer device (see above)

### Communication

- [ ] A code word or phrase with trusted contacts that means "I need help" without saying it, and an agreed action for when they hear it
- [ ] A check-in arrangement: "if you don't hear from me by this time, do this"
- [ ] Know the [Signal for Help](https://canadianwomen.org/signal-for-help/), a one-handed gesture from the Canadian Women's Foundation that asks someone to check in on you safely without leaving a digital trace
- [ ] Know how to reach emergency services where you are, and what you'd say. In most of Canada that's 9-1-1, though [Shelter Safe notes it isn't available in Nunavut](https://sheltersafe.ca/), so check local numbers if you're there

### Documentation

- [ ] A log of incidents: dates, times, what happened, who was involved, any witnesses
- [ ] Saved messages, emails, voicemails and screenshots
- [ ] Kept somewhere the person can't reach. Tech Safety Canada has a free [stalking and technology abuse log](https://techsafety.ca/resources/toolkits/stalking-and-technology-facilitated-abuse-log/) you can use

### Keep it up to date

- [ ] Rehearse it, as Shelter Safe recommends, so you're not reading it for the first time in a crisis
- [ ] Tell the people in your plan that they're in it, and what you'd need from them
- [ ] Review it when something changes: a move, a new job, a new relationship, a court date

## 5. When a plan is about your mental health

*Section last reviewed 12 November 2026.*

If the risk you're planning for is a mental health crisis, start from the Stanley-Brown template on the [9-8-8 website](https://988.ca/get-help/help-right-now). It's free, and it covers the core of the plans tested in the research in section 3: coping strategies, people to contact and, in most of those studies, warning signs. Use its six steps together with the checklist above:

1. **Warning signs:** the thoughts, feelings or situations that tell you a crisis may be coming.
2. **Things you can do on your own** to take your mind off it.
3. **People and places** that give you a distraction.
4. **People you can ask for help.**
5. **Professionals and services** you can contact, including 9-8-8.
6. **Making your surroundings safer.**

9-8-8 says that if you've made a safety plan, follow it, and if you haven't, you can contact them for support. The research in section 3 was on plans made with a clinician, so if you can, make yours with a counsellor, doctor or crisis responder.

Some people hold back from calling a crisis line because they're worried about police turning up. Section 8 notes what each service says about this, so you can choose knowing what to expect.

## 6. Coming out

*Section last reviewed 12 November 2026.*

This is where the conversation started, so it gets its own section. My take: plenty of coming-out conversations go fine, or better than feared, and this section isn't meant to suggest otherwise. But if you're unsure how family, a partner, housemates, your community or your workplace will react, it's worth planning ahead.

It's also a real risk, not just a worry. In the Wathen survey (section 3), respondents who weren't heterosexual were particularly likely to report experiencing domestic violence, and CCOHS lists 2SLGBTQI+ people among the groups at higher risk of intimate partner violence ([CCOHS](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/psychosocial/violence_domestic.html)).

My take on what to add to the general checklist:

- **Pick the timing and the place.** Somewhere you can leave easily, at a time you don't need someone to drive you home, and not right before a holiday you have to spend together.
- **Line up a support person first.** Someone who already knows, who expects to hear from you afterwards, and who'll act if they don't.
- **If you live with the people you're telling,** have a place you could stay for a few nights, and essentials you could take. If you're financially dependent on them, think through what happens if that support were withdrawn.
- **Check your digital trail.** Shared devices, family phone plans and shared streaming or cloud accounts can reveal searches, messages and contacts before you're ready.
- **At work,** you decide who knows. You can ask HR or a manager what they'll do with the information, and who they'd have to tell, before you share it (section 7 explains why they may not be able to keep everything confidential).
- **Have 2SLGBTQI+-specific support saved** (section 8), for peer support afterwards, whatever happens.

## 7. For HR, managers and employers: when someone comes to you

*Section last reviewed 12 November 2026.*

You may never build anyone's safety plan. But you may be one of the people someone tells, or a manager may bring it to you. A few principles:

- **Listen, believe and connect.** Your role isn't to investigate the person's home life or judge their choices. It's to make sure they're safe at work and know where to get specialist help.
- **They lead.** CCOHS says workplace safety plans "must be developed with the consent and at the direction of the person experiencing the violence," updated as things change, and shared only with people who need to know ([CCOHS](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/psychosocial/violence_domestic.html)).
- **Don't promise confidentiality you can't keep.** Be honest at the start about what you're required to pass on and to whom, so they can choose what to share. Duties to report, especially where a child may be at risk, vary by country, province, state and territory. Know yours before you need it.
- **Know your legal duties.** Workplace safety, employment and human rights law can all come into it, and they differ from place to place. In Canada, every province and territory has workplace violence law, some name domestic violence directly, and some offer protected leave; [CCOHS's legislation page](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/psychosocial/violence_legislation.html) is a starting point. Elsewhere, start with your national workplace safety regulator. Check the detail with an adviser.
- **It's fine to be out of your depth.** Your job is to get them to someone who can help, not to be that person yourself. Shelters, victim services, your Employee Assistance Program (EAP) and the services in section 8 do this every day.
- **Train managers.** People tell co-workers and managers far more often than HR (section 3). Make sure managers know what a safety plan is, what to say, and to bring it to you with the person's agreement.
- **Look after the helpers.** Acas, the UK's workplace advisory service, notes that managers and others supporting someone "may be affected themselves" and should be supported throughout ([Acas, 2020](https://www.acas.org.uk/support-in-the-workplace-for-victims-of-domestic-abuse-acas-response)). Point them to the EAP or a crisis line too.

### If you're worried about someone

CCOHS lists signs that someone may be experiencing violence at home, such as suddenly withdrawing from co-workers, frequent disruptive calls or texts from a partner or family member, a partner turning up at work, or sudden changes of address. Its advice for anyone who notices a change: "it is okay to have a private conversation where you name what you have noticed and ask if the individual is okay" ([CCOHS](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/psychosocial/violence_domestic.html)).

My take on using signs like these:

- **They're a reason to check in, not evidence.** Any of them can have other explanations: a disability, being neurodivergent, caring for someone, a health problem, or simply a different way of working. CCOHS's list also includes keeping the camera off in video meetings, which many people do for ordinary reasons. Never record a sign as a concern about someone or act on it as proof.
- **Ask once, kindly and in private.** Name what you've noticed, ask if they're OK, and tell them where help is whether or not they say anything. If they say they're fine, accept that, and let them know they can come back to you any time. Check in again later.
- **If you're worried about suicide, ask directly.** Something like "Are you thinking about suicide?" Section 3 explains why asking is safe. If someone may hurt themselves soon, 9-8-8 says to call 9-1-1 or encourage them to go to the nearest emergency department. If it isn't that urgent, you can call or text 9-8-8 yourself for advice on how to support them ([9-8-8](https://988.ca/get-help/talk-to-someone-you-are-worried-about)). Outside Canada, use your local emergency number and the crisis lines in section 8.

### Scale it to your organisation

Most workplace guidance assumes you have an HR department. Many workplaces don't. Below is my take on what each size of organisation can realistically do, with sources named where they apply. Each stage includes everything in the stages above it.

**Every workplace, of any size**

- **Make help visible.** Put crisis and abuse helpline numbers somewhere people can find them without having to ask, such as a poster in a private spot. The US Workplaces Respond resource centre recommends this for small businesses ([Workplaces Respond](https://workplacesrespond.org/small-business-workplaces/)).
- **Know who you'd call.** Find your local abuse or victim service and crisis line now, before anyone tells you something. Section 8 has starting points.
- **Decide who someone can tell,** and say so to everyone.
- **Nothing happens without the person,** unless someone is in immediate danger (see the principles above).

**No HR person (Workplaces Respond's definition of a small business is about 20 people or fewer)**

- **The owner or most senior manager is the default contact.** If that's awkward, for example because they're close to the person or to the person causing harm, name an outside route as well, such as an EAP if you have one, or a local service.
- **Use what's free.** Workplaces Respond offers small businesses a policy guide, a fill-in policy template and a risk assessment tool, and says it gives no-cost technical assistance. It's American, so check the legal parts for your own country. The rest works anywhere.
- **Use your flexibility.** Changing a shift, a start time or a work location usually costs a small business very little.
- **Privacy is harder in a small team.** Agree with the person exactly what, if anything, colleagues will be told, and in what words.

**One HR person or a small team**

- **Write a short policy.** Acas suggests a domestic abuse policy should set out a clear commitment to taking the issue seriously, common signs of abuse, and the support available for employees and managers. It should be developed with employees and any union or employee representatives, and everyone should be able to find it ([Acas](https://www.acas.org.uk/page-print/1029)).
- **Train managers,** because they're more likely to be told than HR is (section 3).
- **Keep a short, private record.** Acas advises keeping a record of incidents at work, of reports and of actions taken. My take: record what was asked for and what was done, not the details of the abuse; limit who can see it; and tell the person what's written down.
- **Build in leave and flexibility.** The International Labour Organization's Violence and Harassment Recommendation (No. 206) lists measures that could reduce the impact of domestic violence on work, including leave for victims, flexible work arrangements, temporary protection against dismissal, and including domestic violence in workplace risk assessments ([ILO](https://www.ilo.org/resource/questions-answers-0)). In some places some of these are law. Where they aren't, they're still a good place to start.

**An HR function, several sites or several countries**

- **Name and train designated contacts.** In the Canadian survey in section 3, where a workplace had someone designated to handle domestic violence, that person most often helped by developing a safety plan.
- **Put domestic violence in your workplace violence risk assessment,** as ILO Recommendation No. 206 suggests, alongside security and reception procedures (the table below).
- **Check your EAP.** My take: ask whether it covers domestic violence and safety planning, and whether its counsellors know local services in each place you operate.
- **Set one standard, then adapt it for each country.** My take: write a global policy that sets the minimum support everywhere, then keep a one-page sheet for each country with its emergency number, crisis and abuse lines, leave rules and reporting duties. Check each sheet at least once a year, because helpline numbers change.
- **Protect the data.** My take: keep anything about a disclosure out of performance and general HR records, and limit access in your HR system.

### What the workplace can offer

CCOHS lists workplace measures that can form part of a plan, with the person's agreement. The table adapts that list. Leave and flexible work also come from ILO Recommendation No. 206; remote work, marked *, is my addition.

| Area | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Schedule | Flexible hours; changing shifts or start times; avoiding scheduling the person and the abuser together if both work there |
| Location | Moving their desk out of sight of windows or the entrance; remote work*, if it's safer (it isn't always, if the risk is at home) |
| Contact details | Removing them from public directories and websites; a new work number; someone screening calls; blocking the abuser's calls and emails |
| Security | Telling security or reception who to watch for, with a photo or description if the person agrees; a well-lit parking spot near the door; an escort to the car or transit |
| Alarms | The local emergency number saved on work phones; a panic button or personal alarm |
| Legal orders | Asking whether there's a protection or restraining order, and helping make sure its conditions are followed at work |
| Leave and time | Time off, paid if you can, for appointments, court and finding housing; check whether the law where you are requires protected leave |

If the abuser also works for you, CCOHS says to use your disciplinary procedures to hold them accountable for behaviour at work. Get advice before acting.

### What not to do

My take:

- Don't contact the abuser, or confront them for the person.
- Don't make decisions for the person, such as moving them, telling their team or calling police, unless there's immediate danger. CCOHS does list calling the police if the abuser shows criminal behaviour, such as stalking or unauthorized electronic monitoring; agree that step with the person wherever you can.
- Don't treat this as a performance problem. If their work has slipped, deal with safety first.
- Don't write the details into files more people can see than need to.
- Don't make your support depend on the person leaving the relationship, reporting to police or giving you proof. A safety plan covers staying as well as leaving (section 2).

## 8. Resources

*Section last reviewed 12 November 2026. Every number and service below was checked on the service's own website on that date, unless noted.*

Canada comes first because that's where this volume started and where its list is fullest. Other countries follow, then a directory for everywhere else.

### Canada: right now

| Service | How to reach it | Good to know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [9-8-8: Suicide Crisis Helpline](https://988.ca/) | Call or text 9-8-8, 24/7 | For anyone in Canada. Partners include Hope for Wellness and Kids Help Phone. Deaf and hard-of-hearing people can use Canada VRS, TTY (dial 7-1-1) or IP Relay. 9-8-8 says it involves emergency services only in a very small number of cases, when someone is at imminent risk, and tries other options first, but that it can't promise it won't call 9-1-1 ([what to expect](https://988.ca/get-help/what-to-expect)) |
| Emergency services | 9-1-1, or your local emergency number | If you or someone else is in immediate danger. [Shelter Safe](https://sheltersafe.ca/) notes 9-1-1 isn't available in Nunavut, and in May 2025 [Nunatsiaq News reported](https://nunatsiaq.com/?p=232185) the territory's minister saying it wouldn't arrive in the near future. If you're there, save your community's local emergency numbers |
| [Kids Help Phone](https://kidshelpphone.ca/) | Call 1-800-668-6868 or text CONNECT to 686868, 24/7 | For young people. Free and confidential. Indigenous youth can text FIRST NATIONS, INUIT or METIS to 686868 to ask for an Indigenous crisis responder, if one is available |
| [Hope for Wellness Helpline](https://www.hopeforwellness.ca/) | Call 1-855-242-3310 or chat online, 24/7 | For all Indigenous people across Canada |
| 211 | Call 2-1-1 or visit 211.ca | Finds local services such as counselling. 9-8-8 describes it as free, confidential and available 24/7 in most of Canada |

Some lists still online give 1-833-456-4566 or text 45645, under the name Crisis Services Canada or Talk Suicide Canada. Use 9-8-8 instead.

### Canada: 2SLGBTQI+ support

| Service | How to reach it | Good to know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Trans Lifeline](https://translifeline.org/hotline/) | Canada: 1-877-330-6366 | Peer support run by and for trans people. Monday to Friday, 1 pm to 9 pm Eastern. Says it does no non-consensual active rescue (calling 911 or police without your agreement), but reserves the right to report credible threats of violence to others and suspected child abuse |
| [LGBT YouthLine](https://www.youthline.ca/helpline/peer-support-helpline/) | Text 647-694-4275, or chat on the website | For people 29 and under in Ontario, with trained 2SLGBTQ+ peer volunteers. Peer support, not crisis support. Sunday to Friday, 4 pm to 9:30 pm Eastern. Stopped taking calls in 2023, so phone numbers on older lists won't reach the help line |
| [pflag Canada](https://pflagcanada.ca/) | [Local chapters](https://pflagcanada.ca/pflag-canada-chapters/) | Peer support for 2SLGBTQIA+ people and their families. Not a crisis service: its contact page points people to 9-8-8 or Kids Help Phone (its FAQ still gives the old number below) |
| [Egale Canada](https://egale.ca/) | [Find Support](https://egale.ca/resources/#category=resources&topics=support-navigation) directory | National 2SLGBTQI organisation for research, education and advocacy, with a directory of support services. Its [history page](https://egale.ca/about/) describes youth counselling and crisis services for people up to 29, launched in 2014; check what's running now |
| [It Gets Better Canada](https://itgetsbettercanada.org/get-help/) | Get Help directory | A directory of 2SLGBTQ+ services across Canada, by province. Some numbers on it are out of date (including the old Crisis Services Canada number), so check the service's own site |

### Canada: abuse and violence

Abuse isn't limited by gender, but many specialist services are. Shelter Safe and Tech Safety Canada come from Women's Shelters Canada, and some of the provincial lines Shelter Safe lists are for women only. Tech Safety Canada says its toolkit is for women, youth and gender-diverse people. If you're a man, or a shelter isn't the right fit, start with the Canadian Resource Centre for Victims of Crime or local victim services (below), 2-1-1, or 9-8-8 if you're in crisis.

| Service | How to reach it | Good to know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Shelter Safe](https://sheltersafe.ca/) | Map of shelters and transition houses, plus [provincial and territorial help lines](https://sheltersafe.ca/about/find-help/) | Run by Women's Shelters Canada; mostly women's shelters. Some listed lines are for women only; others, such as VictimLink BC, aren't described that way. Shelter staff help with safety plans, 24/7, by phone, text and email. Tech Safety Canada adds that you don't need to stay in a shelter to get free, confidential support |
| [Tech Safety Canada](https://techsafety.ca/) | Online toolkits | Planning around phones, accounts, location tracking and stalkerware. Written for women, youth and gender-diverse people, but the technical steps apply to anyone. Read it on a safer device |
| [DomesticShelters.org](https://www.domesticshelters.org/) | Searchable directory | Covers Canada and the US. Lists Canadian provincial groups |
| [Signal for Help](https://canadianwomen.org/signal-for-help/) | A hand signal | From the Canadian Women's Foundation. Also teaches others how to respond if they see it |

### Canada: workplace harassment and discrimination

- [CCOHS: Intimate partner and family violence at work](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/psychosocial/violence_domestic.html), and its [legislation page](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/psychosocial/violence_legislation.html) by jurisdiction. Free.
- [Ontario Human Rights Commission: Policy on discrimination and harassment because of sexual orientation](https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation), and [Policy on preventing discrimination because of gender identity and gender expression](https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/policy-preventing-discrimination-because-gender-identity-and-gender-expression). Ontario-specific; other provinces and territories have their own human rights bodies.
- Federally regulated workplaces: the [Work Place Harassment and Violence Prevention Regulations](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2020-130/index.html) under the Canada Labour Code.

### Canada: stalking and criminal harassment

- Criminal harassment is an offence under [section 264 of the Criminal Code](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-46/section-264.html). Whether something meets that bar is a question for police or a lawyer.
- [Canadian Resource Centre for Victims of Crime](https://crcvc.ca/): 1-877-232-2610. Free, confidential, bilingual support and advocacy for victims of serious crime.
- CCOHS suggests getting expert advice on safety planning from a local women's shelter, a victim services organisation or the police. Your local police non-emergency line can point you to victim services.

Emergency: 911 |

Emergency: 000 |

### Everywhere else

- [Find A Helpline](https://findahelpline.com/): a free directory of crisis and support lines by country and topic, including domestic violence and 2SLGBTQI+ support. The International Association for Suicide Prevention partners with it and describes it as covering verified helplines in over 150 countries ([IASP](https://www.iasp.info/crisis-centres/)).
- [Befrienders Worldwide](https://befrienders.org/): emotional support centres in 32 countries, according to IASP.

## 9. A blank plan you can copy

*Section last reviewed 12 November 2026.*

Copy this somewhere the person you're worried about can't see. A paper copy kept at a trusted person's home works, or at work if the person you're worried about can't get to it there. If your situation is mainly about your mental health, use the [9-8-8 template](https://988.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/9-8-8-Safety-Plan-Template-English.pdf) as well.

```
MY SAFETY PLAN                        Last updated: ____________

PEOPLE
Trusted contact:        ______________________  Phone: ____________
Backup contact:         ______________________  Phone: ____________
Out-of-area contact:    ______________________  Phone: ____________
Professional(s):        ______________________  Phone: ____________

PLACES
If I need to leave, I'll go to:  _________________________________
Backup place:                    _________________________________
Nearest shelter / crisis centre: _________________________________

ESSENTIALS
Copies of documents are kept at:  ________________________________
Emergency money is:               ________________________________
My bag / list is:                 ________________________________
Medications and daily needs:      ________________________________
Children, pets, dependants:       ________________________________

SIGNALS
Code word or phrase:  ____________  It means my contact should: ___
Check-in: if ________ doesn't hear from me by ______, they will: __

NUMBERS                       (in Canada: 9-1-1 and 9-8-8; see section 8)
Emergency: ______________   Crisis line: ______________
Abuse helpline: _________   Other: ___________________

RECORDS
My incident log is kept at: ______________________________________

BEFORE I CHANGE PASSWORDS, LOCATION OR ACCOUNTS
I've documented what's happening:  yes / no
I've talked it through with:       _______________________________
```

### Sources

Free to read, as far as we could check. Services' own websites are listed in section 8.

- [Nuij, van Ballegooijen, Juniar, Erlangsen, Portzky, O'Connor, Smit, Kerkhof and Riper: Safety planning-type interventions for suicide prevention: meta-analysis](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/238354/) (*British Journal of Psychiatry*, 2021, [doi:10.1192/bjp.2021.50](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.2021.50)). Peer-reviewed meta-analysis. Free copy via the University of Glasgow.
- [Albaum, Irwin, Muha, Schumacher, Clarissa, Finkelstein, Bridge and Korczak: Safety Planning Interventions for Suicide Prevention in Children and Adolescents: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2025.1012) (*JAMA Pediatrics*, 2025, 179(8), 886–895). Peer-reviewed systematic review and meta-analysis. A free copy is listed on PubMed Central (PMC12090068); see also the [SickKids summary](https://www.sickkids.ca/en/news/archive/2025/study-investigates-efficacy-of-safety-planning-interventions-for-suicide-prevention-among-children-and-adolescents/).
- [Rivas, Ramsay, Sadowski, Davidson, Dunnes, Eldridge, Hegarty, Taft and Feder: Advocacy interventions to reduce or eliminate violence and promote the physical and psychosocial well-being of women who experience intimate partner abuse](https://doi.org/10.4073/csr.2016.2) (*Campbell Systematic Reviews*, 2016, 12(1)). Peer-reviewed systematic review. Open access.
- [Ford-Gilboe, Varcoe, Scott-Storey, Perrin, Wuest, Wathen, Case and Glass: Longitudinal impacts of an online safety and health intervention for women experiencing intimate partner violence: randomized controlled trial](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-020-8152-8) (*BMC Public Health*, 2020, 20, 260). Peer-reviewed randomised trial. Open access (CC BY 4.0).
- [Dazzi, Gribble, Wessely and Fear: Does asking about suicide and related behaviours induce suicidal ideation? What is the evidence?](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291714001299) (*Psychological Medicine*, 2014, 44, 3361–3363). Peer-reviewed editorial with a literature review. The publisher's PDF opened without a login when checked.
- [Wathen, MacGregor and MacQuarrie, with the Canadian Labour Congress: Can Work Be Safe, When Home Isn't?](https://www.uwo.ca/projects/heritage/heritage3/img/survey-report.pdf) (2014). Initial findings of a pan-Canadian survey. Research report; non-random sample recruited mainly through unions.
- [International Labour Organization: Questions and answers on Convention No. 190 and Recommendation No. 206](https://www.ilo.org/resource/questions-answers-0), and the [Violence and Harassment Recommendation, 2019 (No. 206)](https://www.ilo.org/resource/ilc/108/violence-and-harassment-recommendation-2019-no-206). International labour standard.
- Acas: [Domestic violence and abuse guidance for employers](https://www.acas.org.uk/page-print/1029) and [response to the UK government consultation on workplace support for victims of domestic abuse](https://www.acas.org.uk/support-in-the-workplace-for-victims-of-domestic-abuse-acas-response) (2020). UK public body guidance.
- [Workplaces Respond to Domestic and Sexual Violence: Small Business Workplaces](https://workplacesrespond.org/small-business-workplaces/). US national resource centre funded by the Office on Violence Against Women, US Department of Justice.
- [9-8-8: Talk to someone you are worried about](https://988.ca/get-help/talk-to-someone-you-are-worried-about). Service website.
- [International Association for Suicide Prevention: crisis centres](https://www.iasp.info/crisis-centres/). Professional association website.
- [Nunatsiaq News: Next-generation 9-1-1 service not coming to Nunavut any time soon](https://nunatsiaq.com/?p=232185) (30 May 2025). News report.
- [9-8-8: Suicide Crisis Helpline](https://988.ca/): [help right now](https://988.ca/get-help/help-right-now), [what to expect](https://988.ca/get-help/what-to-expect), [safety plan template](https://988.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/9-8-8-Safety-Plan-Template-English.pdf) (Stanley and Brown). Service website.
