1. Who this is for, and what it isn't#

Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 20 November 2026.

Who it's for: everyone. This section tells you where to start.

If you've been asked to "do something about menopause", or someone on your team has quietly mentioned their periods are making work hard, and you're not sure what good looks like, this is for you. I'm writing for founders, People and HR leads, line managers and colleagues, and for the people living with this themselves.

It covers periods, perimenopause and menopause, and the conditions that sit alongside them, like endometriosis, PCOS and PMDD. It's for anyone who menstruates or goes through menopause, whatever their gender identity or other factors. That includes trans men and non-binary people, and people whose menopause came early or was brought on by surgery or medical treatment. It's also written for the managers and colleagues who don't go through any of this themselves, because they're often the ones who most need to understand it.

The idea this whole volume starts from is simple: the people living with this usually know best what helps them. The organisation's job is to make sure they know what's possible, make asking easy, and make support work without anyone having to explain themselves.

Does any of this sound familiar?#

These are illustrative, not real companies, but you'll probably recognise at least one.

  • You're the founder of a 30-person company. Someone has asked whether you have a menopause policy. You don't, and you're not sure you need one at your size.
  • You're the People lead at a 250-person company. Leadership wants a menopause policy by next quarter, and you've been sent three templates that all say slightly different things.
  • You're a manager. One of your team keeps calling in sick for a day or two at a time, roughly once a month. You've been told to "keep an eye on attendance".
  • You're living with this. Some weeks are fine and some aren't, and you don't want to explain your body to your manager to get a later start.

Each of those needs something different, and the sections below are how to give it to them.

Find your way in#

  • Founders and leaders: start with section 2, on what the evidence shows, then section 9 on how you'd know it's working.
  • People and HR leads: sections 4 to 7 are the build. Section 6 looks at three published policies side by side. Section 10 has templates.
  • Line managers: sections 4 and 5 are what you'll actually do. Section 7 covers the mistakes that are easiest to make without meaning to.
  • If you're living with this: section 8 is written for you.

What this isn't#

It isn't medical advice. I'm not a clinician, and this guide doesn't recommend treatments, supplements, hormone therapy or ways to manage symptoms. Where it describes a condition, it uses a clinical body's own words and points you there. If you're worried about your health, talk to a doctor or another qualified health professional.

It isn't legal advice either. A lot of countries now have laws or official guidance that touch on this, from employment and equality law to menstrual leave. They change, and they differ a lot from place to place, so check locally before you write or change a policy.

It doesn't cover pregnancy, pregnancy loss, fertility or testosterone decline in cis men. Those deserve their own volume, and they'll get one.

And it isn't a ranking of anyone else's work. Section 6 looks at three published policies. Each was written for its own company, and I've tried to say what each does well before I say what I'd do differently and why.

How sure am I?#

Everything in here is one of three kinds, and I've tried to make it obvious which without tagging every line. If there's a link, there's a source behind it, and the text says what kind of source it is. If it's my view, I'll say so in plain words.

This topic needs one more thing spelt out, because a lot of what's written about it comes from brands, influencers and surveys run by people selling something. I only use three kinds of source:

  1. Peer-reviewed research, for any claim about what's happening or what works.
  2. Clinical bodies, like the World Health Organization, for what a condition is. Never for advice.
  3. Governments and standards bodies, only to show you what exists, never as proof that something works.

Two other kinds appear, and they're always labelled: one large survey report, used for figures nobody else has measured, and published policies, used as examples of practice in section 6. Blogs, influencers and vendor posts stay out.

Label What it means An example from this playbook
Evidenced There's research behind it, and it's cited Working conditions, like role clarity and support, predicted work outcomes better than menopausal stage did
Practitioner consensus At least two published policies or practitioner sources do it, and they're linked. Nobody's studied it properly Offering flexible hours and working from home on bad days (two of the policies in section 6 do this)

When the evidence is thin I'll tell you, and on this topic that's often. Most of the research comes from white, cisgender women in wealthy countries (section 2 sets out the gaps), and there's very little testing of whether workplace support actually works. I'd rather you know that than have me sound confident.

2. Why it matters: what the evidence shows, and what it doesn't#

Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 20 November 2026.

Who it's for: founders and leaders who want to understand what's actually going on, and anyone explaining it to them.

Symptoms from periods, perimenopause and menopause are real, common and can make work harder. But the best evidence I've found says the working environment shapes how people get on at work more than their menopausal stage does. That suggests a lot of what happens at work is shaped by the workplace, which makes it something a workplace can act on, not a performance risk to manage. The rest of this section is the evidence for that, including where it runs out.

Periods and work#

The largest study I've found is a Dutch survey of 32,748 women aged 15 to 45. 13.8% had missed work or school because of their period, about 1.3 days a year on average. Far more common was working while unwell: 80.7% said they'd done it, adding up to an average of 8.9 days of lost productivity a year. Only one in five of those who called in sick told their employer the real reason. Two-thirds said they wished they had more flexibility in their tasks and working hours during their period (Schoep et al., 2019). People were recruited through social media, so it's a self-selected sample, and I'd treat the numbers as a strong signal rather than an exact rate.

What stands out to me is the hidden part. Most of the cost is people at their desks, not people at home, and most of the absence isn't recorded as what it is. A policy that only counts sick days is looking at the smallest part of this.

Menopause and work#

The most quoted number here is that one in ten women have left a job because of menopause symptoms. It comes from a survey of 4,014 UK women aged 45 to 55, weighted to represent that age group, run by Savanta ComRes for a Channel 4 documentary, with the Fawcett Society (Fawcett, 2022). It's a solid survey for what it is. It doesn't follow people over time, so it can't show cause, and it left out women whose menopause came early and women over 55. The finding I'd point you to first is the disability gap: 22% of disabled women said they'd left a job because of menopause symptoms, compared with 9% of non-disabled women.

The study that changed how I've framed this section looked at it from the other side. An online survey of 216 women aged 45 to 60, all members of a UK union and professional association for family court and probation staff, found that work outcomes weren't linked to menopausal status. They were linked to job stress and the working environment, especially how clear people's roles were and how much control and support they had. Having hot flushes wasn't linked to work outcomes either. The one exception was how problematic hot flushes were at work, which went with intending to stop working altogether (Hardy et al., 2018). The authors concluded that menopause did not affect women's self-reported performance or absence. The sample was mostly white, degree-educated and in office jobs, and everything was self-reported, so it's one study, not the final word.

A 2025 systematic review lands in a similar place. From its abstract, it found that an "ideal worker" standard at work can feel like a threat to people going through menopause, and that workplace factors can make that feeling worse or ease it. How people cope depends on how much say they have over their own work (Rowson and Jones, 2025).

Put together, I read it like this: the symptoms are real, and for some people they're hard to work through. But whether that becomes a problem at work depends a lot on the job around them. That's the part you control.

Who the research leaves out#

Most of what we know comes from white, cisgender women, and it's worth being honest about that before you build anything on it.

  • Trans and non-binary people. The Fawcett survey had no usable sample of trans or non-binary people. A 2023 review of workplace policies found none that addressed their experiences at work, or those of intersex people (Howe et al., 2023). WHO's own menopause fact sheet notes that trans men and some non-binary people go through menopause, and says it uses "women" because most data doesn't record gender identity (WHO). One recent interview study of trans and gender diverse people who menstruate describes fear of being misgendered, limited access to products, and toilets that don't work for them (Pasmanns et al., 2026).
  • Ethnicity. A US study running for over 15 years found frequent hot flushes and night sweats lasted a median of 7.4 years overall, but 10.1 years for African American women, 8.9 for Hispanic women, 6.5 for non-Hispanic white women, 5.4 for Chinese women and 4.8 for Japanese women (Avis et al., 2015, free copy; publisher). Fawcett's ethnic subgroups were too small to rely on, and it said so. So when you see a "typical" timeline, ask whose timeline it is.
  • People whose menopause came early, or was caused by surgery or treatment. They're often outside the age bands studies use.
  • People who don't know yet. WHO estimates up to 70% of people with PCOS are undiagnosed (WHO). Endometriosis can take years to diagnose (WHO). You can't disclose what you haven't been told.

This is why I'd write any policy for everyone it could apply to, and never make support depend on someone telling you who they are or what they have. Not knowing who's affected doesn't mean nobody is. It means your support has to work without anyone having to say so.

Where the popular numbers come from#

If you've been handed a slide on this, you've probably seen some of these. I'm not saying any are false. I'm saying what they actually count.

You'll see Where it comes from What that means for you

The authors think the confirmed figures probably underestimate how many people have PMDD at some point in their lives, and that the provisional figure probably overestimates it

If you've got the original source for a figure that isn't here, I'd like to see it, and I'll add it.

How sure is this?#

The evidence that symptoms affect people at work is decent and comes from large surveys, though most of it is self-reported. The evidence that the working environment matters more than menopausal stage rests on one well-designed but small study, plus a review that points the same way. Almost all of it comes from white, cisgender women in wealthy countries, and I've tried to say so each time it matters.

What would change my mind: good evidence, from a wider range of people and workplaces, that menopausal stage itself predicts work outcomes once job stress and working conditions are taken into account. If that turned up, I'd reframe this section around the person rather than the job.

3. What we're actually talking about#

Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 20 November 2026.

Who it's for: anyone who wants a plain starting point, especially managers and colleagues who've never had to think about this.

This section is a short reference, not a medical guide. Each entry says in plain words what a condition is, using a clinical body's own description, and why it matters at work. It doesn't list every symptom, and it doesn't say anything about treatment. That's on purpose. Your job isn't to recognise or diagnose anything. It's to know these things exist, that they can be serious, and that many people living with them don't have a diagnosis yet.

A note on words. Most clinical sources say "women", because most of the data doesn't record gender identity. WHO says so itself, and notes that trans men and some non-binary people go through menopause too (WHO). Where I give a figure, it's for the group that was measured. Everywhere else, I mean anyone it applies to.

What it is In plain words Why it matters at work
Periods and period pain Painful periods cause crampy pain in the lower abdomen, sometimes with back or leg pain. Pain that starts later in life, or changes, can be linked to conditions like endometriosis or fibroids (MedlinePlus) MedlinePlus calls painful periods the leading cause of lost time from school and work for young women in their teens and 20s. Most of the effect is people working while unwell, not time off (section 2)
Perimenopause The transition before menopause. It starts with the first signs, often changes to the menstrual cycle, and ends a year after the final period. It can last several years (WHO) My view is that its effects, like poor sleep, can easily be put down to something else, and that support which waits for someone to name the cause may come too late
Menopause Confirmed after 12 months in a row without a period, with no other cause. Most people reach it between 45 and 55. Common symptoms include hot flushes, night sweats, sleep problems and mood changes (WHO) WHO notes that menopause is often not discussed at work
Early and induced menopause Menopause before 40 is called premature. Induced menopause is brought on by surgery that removes both ovaries, or by treatments like chemotherapy or radiotherapy (WHO) It can happen to people much younger than anyone expects, and sometimes suddenly. Age-based assumptions miss them
Endometriosis A long-term condition where tissue similar to the lining of the womb grows outside it, causing inflammation and scarring. It affects roughly 10% of women of reproductive age, about 190 million people, and can affect trans men and non-binary people who menstruate. Diagnosis takes 4 to 12 years on average (WHO) Some people have pain severe enough to stop them working. With diagnosis taking years, many of the people affected won't have a name for it yet
PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome) A common hormonal condition that can disrupt periods, ovulation and fertility. An estimated 10–13% of women of reproductive age have it, and up to 70% are undiagnosed (WHO) WHO notes it's linked to anxiety and depression, and that the stigma around some of its effects can affect work
PMS and PMDD PMS is a range of physical or emotional symptoms in the days before a period, easing once it starts. PMDD is a related condition with similar symptoms that can be disabling, and it always includes at least one mood-related symptom (MedlinePlus). Around 1.6% to 3.2% of people who menstruate have PMDD, depending on how it's measured (section 2) It's cyclical, so someone can be fine for weeks and then struggle for a few days every month. That pattern is easy to misread as a performance or attendance problem

This isn't a complete list, and it isn't meant to be. Fibroids, adenomyosis, heavy menstrual bleeding and other conditions affect people at work too. If someone tells you about one that isn't here, the support is the same: listen, ask what would help, and keep it private.

Any of these can be serious#

Any of the conditions in this section can be life-altering, even devastating, for the person living with it, and deserves to be treated that seriously. How serious it is for someone isn't something you can judge from the outside, and this guide doesn't rank one condition above another. Take what someone tells you at face value. One example a clinical body names: some people with PMDD have thoughts of suicide (MedlinePlus).

You don't need to know more than that to act well. If someone tells you they're having thoughts of suicide, take it seriously, don't leave them alone if they're in immediate danger, and help them reach your local emergency number or a crisis line. Then involve whoever in your organisation supports wellbeing, with their agreement. That's true whatever the cause. That last step is my view rather than the clinical source's.

How sure is this?#

Everything in this section comes from clinical bodies, the World Health Organization and the US National Library of Medicine's MedlinePlus, plus one figure from the research in section 2. It's descriptive, not a finding about what works. I've kept it to what those sources say and stopped there, because the rest is for clinicians.

What would change my mind: clinical bodies changing their descriptions or figures. When they do, this section gets updated to match.

4. What helps#

Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 20 November 2026.

Who it's for: People and HR leads deciding what to offer, and managers putting it into practice.

Most of what helps is ordinary: some flexibility over time and place, decent facilities, a sensible way of handling absence, and knowing where to find support. None of it needs a diagnosis. The bigger decisions are how you offer it, and there's no single right way. This section gives you the choices and what each one costs, so you can pick what fits your people.

What people say they need#

The research here is mostly about what people ask for, not about what's been tested. It's consistent, though.

  • For menopause, a survey of 896 women aged 45 to 55 in professional, managerial and admin jobs across 10 UK organisations found four areas where employers could help: managers knowing more about menopause, flexible working hours, access to information and support at work, and attention to temperature and ventilation. Most of the women hadn't told their line managers, who were mostly men or younger than them (Griffiths, MacLennan and Hassard, 2013, free copy; publisher).
  • For periods, two-thirds of the women in the Dutch survey in section 2 said they wanted more flexibility in their tasks and working hours during their period (Schoep et al., 2019).
  • For periods and gynaecological conditions, a study of 627 people working in higher education, including trans and non-binary people, describes the extra work people do to manage pain and bleeding, get to adequate facilities, deal with stigma and keep on top of their workload. The authors argue that this currently falls on each person to sort out alone, and that the responsibility should move to the organisation (Sang et al., 2021).
  • For trans and gender diverse people, an interview study recommends period products and disposal bins in all toilets, and gender-neutral language (Pasmanns et al., 2026).

Choices you'll make#

The trade-offs in this table are my reading, not findings. Where there's evidence, it's linked.

Choice Options What each one costs you
How support is offered Available to everyone by default, like flexible hours and working from home on bad days. Or available on request, with a light process Default for everyone means nobody has to explain themselves, and it helps people who don't know what's causing their symptoms yet. It can be harder for roles with fixed hours or on-site work. On request lets you tailor support to the job, but it only reaches people willing to ask, and the survey above found most hadn't told their manager anything
Leave for periods Dedicated menstrual leave. Or flexible sick leave and working arrangements that cover it without naming it Dedicated leave is visible and shows you take it seriously. It also means naming the reason, and research on public attitudes found worries that it would make women look bad and a theme the researchers called "What do men get?" (Barnack-Tavlaris et al., 2019). One argument is that without tackling the stigma underneath, it could backfire on the people it's meant to help (Levitt and Barnack-Tavlaris, 2020, free copy; publisher). Flexible sick leave keeps the reason private, but it relies on managers applying it fairly. I haven't found any research on what either does to outcomes
Attendance triggers Don't count absence linked to these conditions. Or keep your usual triggers, with a manager review before any action. Or don't use triggers at all Not counting it protects people whose absence comes in a monthly or recurring pattern. It only works if they've told someone the reason, which many won't. A manager review keeps the system but depends on judgement, which brings its own bias. Dropping triggers altogether avoids both, but leaves you without an early warning for other problems
Whether people have to say why No reason needed. Or a reason given to one named person in confidence No reason needed is simplest and most private. One sample policy in section 6 says telling your manager you're unwell is enough. Asking for a reason lets you spot patterns and offer more, but it puts people off, and the people least likely to say are often the ones you most need to reach
Small or temporary changes No formal process. Or the same process as any other adjustment No formal process is quicker, and symptoms often come and go. A formal process gives you a record and consistency. One sample policy in section 6 uses no process for small or temporary changes and its standard one for permanent changes

You don't have to pick one column for everything. Plenty of organisations will offer some things by default and others on request. What matters is that you've chosen on purpose and written it down.

Things that work whichever way you go#

These came up across the research and the sample policies, and they don't depend on anyone telling you anything:

  • Products and disposal bins in every toilet, not just the women's.
  • Some control over temperature and air, like a desk fan, a window, or a cooler place to work.
  • Easy access to toilets and breaks, without having to ask or explain.
  • Clear information about what's available, in one place, that people can find without asking anyone.
  • Managers who know the basics, covered in section 5.

The menopause study in section 5 adds a few more: cold drinking water, somewhere to rest, and looser uniforms where people wear one (Hardy, Griffiths and Hunter, 2017).

On appointments, my take is simple. Treat them like any other medical appointment. Whatever your policy says about time off, notes and protections for a doctor's appointment applies here too, no more and no less. Don't ask for extra proof, and don't make people use annual leave for it. What protections apply to medical appointments differs from country to country, so check locally.

If people work remotely, the facilities matter less and the flexibility matters more.

How sure is this?#

What people say they need is reasonably well documented, mostly in the UK, with one large Dutch study, though most samples are office-based, and the ones that report ethnicity are mostly white. Whether any of it works is barely tested. A 2023 systematic review found only five studies of workplace support for menopause, mostly small and with selected groups of people (Rodrigo et al., 2023). For menstrual leave, there's debate but no outcome evidence that I could find. That's why I've given you choices rather than a recommendation.

What would change my mind: well-run comparisons showing one approach, like dedicated menstrual leave or default flexibility, gets better results than the others for the people it's meant to help. If that turns up, the matching choice would get a clear recommendation.

5. The conversation#

Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 20 November 2026.

Who it's for: line managers first, then People and HR teams, and anyone someone might choose to confide in.

The person leads. Your job is to make telling someone safe, optional and simple, and to make support work for people who never tell anyone at all. Most of what follows comes from what people living with this have said they want, and it's mostly common sense done carefully.

Before anyone says anything#

Most people won't tell their manager. In the UK survey in section 4, most of the women hadn't, and their managers were mostly men or younger than them (Griffiths, MacLennan and Hassard, 2013). So don't build support that only starts once someone discloses.

In practice that means:

  • Say where to go, in writing, so nobody has to ask how to ask.
  • Offer more than one person to talk to. A manager, someone in HR, someone in another team. Whoever the person feels comfortable with.
  • Offer to start the conversation for them. One sample policy in section 6 offers to help someone raise it with their manager, rather than leaving them to start it alone (Open Org template).

Who someone talks to#

In a UK study of 137 working women, some said they'd feel more comfortable talking about menopause with a female manager (Hardy, Griffiths and Hunter, 2017, free copy). That's worth knowing, but it isn't true for everyone, and it doesn't account for trans and non-binary people at all.

My take is that the choice belongs to the person. Whether that's their manager, someone in HR, or a trusted colleague in a different department, the route should be open to them. You still need an escalation path for when something has to go further. If people aren't getting the support they need, look at your own routes to see where the gaps are. But someone's dignity comes before any manager's sense that they ought to be told first.

When someone tells you#

The women in that study were clear about what helped and what didn't. These aren't a script, just what they described.

What helps What gets in the way
Listening carefully, with empathy and respect Assuming any difficulty must be caused by their condition
Open questions that let them lead, without pressure to share more Generalising from someone else's experience, or a one-size-fits-all fix
Keeping it private unless they say otherwise Drawing attention to their symptoms in front of others
Knowing the basics, like how bad nights can affect the next day Jokes, flippancy, or being patronising
Remembering it varies from person to person, and support may only be needed for a while Turning it into a big issue with lots of questions, or avoiding it entirely

Source for the table: Hardy, Griffiths and Hunter, 2017.

Some examples of the difference. These are illustrations, not lines to learn.

Gets in the way Leaves room for them to lead
"Is this a hormone thing?" "Thanks for telling me. What would make the next few weeks easier?"
"My partner had that too, she found yoga really helped." "What's worked for you before, at work or anywhere else?"
"Let's get you some time off." "Would anything about how or when you work help? You decide what we try."
"I'll let the team know so they can be understanding." "Who, if anyone, would you like to know about this?"

Keeping it private#

Agree with the person what gets written down, who sees it, and why. Record only what's needed to put support in place. If they'd rather nothing about their condition is written down, record the adjustment, not the reason. Rules on health information differ from country to country, so check locally what applies to you.

Not a performance issue#

A symptom isn't a verdict on someone's ability. In the study from section 2, menopausal stage wasn't linked to how people rated their own performance or absence. How clear their role was, and how stressful the job was, mattered more (Hardy et al., 2018).

There are two ways to get this wrong. One is assuming that someone who's struggling must be struggling because of their condition. The other is starting a formal performance process without ever asking whether something else is going on. The women in the 2017 study warned about the second one specifically. One sample policy in section 6 puts the first one plainly: don't treat a disclosure as a sign of future performance.

The way through both is the same. Don't assume, and ask before you act.

Colleagues#

Most of what colleagues need to know is short. Don't joke about it, don't comment on anyone's symptoms, and don't pass on what someone has told you. If someone tells you something in confidence, that's theirs to share, not yours.

How sure is this?#

The table of what helps comes from one study of 137 women in one mostly office-based, highly educated workforce, and the authors say so. It fits with the larger survey from section 4 and with the sample policies, but it hasn't been tested as a way of managing. Nothing here comes from trans or non-binary people, and that's a real gap. The parts on who someone talks to, keeping it private and colleagues are my view, apart from the point about jokes, which comes from the study.

What would change my mind: evidence from a wider range of people, especially trans and non-binary people and people in manual or shift work, that they want something different from these conversations. If that turns up, this section changes to match.

6. Writing a policy: three published examples#

Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 20 November 2026.

Who it's for: People and HR leads writing or reviewing a policy, and founders writing their first one.

The quickest way to write a good policy is to read a few real ones. These are three that companies have published openly, which I found through Open Org. Each was written for its own company, at its own size and moment, and each gets things right that a lot of policies don't. I'm not ranking them, and they're in alphabetical order for that reason. For each, I'll say what it does well first, then what I'd do differently and why. Take what fits your people.

At a glance#

Changes to how you work Temporary or permanent changes to hours, timings or responsibilities Flexible working on bad days, and regular breaks Later starts, working from home, a cooler space or fan, extra breaks, temporary workload changes. No formal process for small or temporary ones
Who to talk to Your manager first, or People Ops if you can't The People team, for specific needs Your manager, or a named People contact. They'll also help you start the conversation
Medical input May ask you to consent to an examination by a doctor it nominates, with the report shared with the company and its advisers Not covered With your consent, occupational health or your own doctor, with costs covered
Facilities Not covered Products in the bathrooms at its London office Not covered
Further support Mental health policy, employee assistance programme, short-term therapy Health and fitness budget Employee assistance programme, if there is one, and a list of UK organisations

Sources: Human Made, Juro, Open Org template. All read on 19 November 2026.

Human Made#

What it does well. It names trans and non-binary people from the start, folds perimenopause in rather than leaving it out, and lists a wide range of symptoms, including memory, concentration, depression and anxiety. It gives a second route through People Ops for anyone who can't talk to their manager, allows for both temporary and permanent changes, and links straight to counselling and other support. It's also openly licensed under CC BY-SA 4.0, the same licence as this playbook.

What I'd do differently. It asks people to tell their manager when an absence is menopause-related, so it can be recorded properly. That's a reasonable choice, and it lets the company spot patterns and offer more. But it's the opposite of the no-reason-needed route, and section 4 sets out what each one costs. On the medical examination, it asks for consent, which matters. I'd go further and say exactly what the report will contain, who will see it and why, and point people to local data protection rules.

Juro#

What it does well. It covers periods as well as menopause, which most policies don't, and it counts period problems under trust-based paid sick leave rather than making people find another way to take the day. It offers flexible working on bad days, provides products at its office, and invites people to tell the People team how to improve it. Its figure for endometriosis matches WHO's.

What I'd do differently. Its note on language includes trans men and non-binary people, then says the word "women" means anyone who identifies as female. Those two point in different directions, so I'd keep to one clear definition. It also has a short callout suggesting that learning to manage your cycle might reduce symptoms instead of medication. This playbook stays out of health advice altogether, so I'd leave that kind of suggestion to clinicians. And it quotes a market-size figure from an investor's blog post, where I'd stick to clinical and peer-reviewed sources. It also covers pregnancy and pregnancy loss, which this volume leaves for another one, so that's a different scope rather than something to change.

Open Org template#

What it does well. It covers the whole menopause transition, says outright that gender identity doesn't matter, and includes contractors. Nobody has to say why they're off, and menopause-related absence doesn't count in attendance reviews. It offers to help someone start the conversation with their manager, gives managers a short, practical checklist, and covers the cost of occupational health.

What I'd do differently. It's about menopause only, so periods and related conditions would need adding. Its list of organisations is all UK-based, so readers elsewhere will need their own. And as a template, how good it ends up depends on how the gaps get filled in. That's true of any template, not a fault in this one.

What all three leave out#

None of them says how you'd know whether the policy is working, and none points to workplace research behind what it offers. That's normal for a policy, which isn't a research paper. But it's the gap this playbook tries to fill. Section 9 covers how to tell whether yours is working.

How sure is this?#

This section describes three documents as they read on 19 November 2026. Policies change, so check the current versions before you borrow from them. The "what I'd do differently" parts are my view, tied to the evidence and principles in sections 2 to 5.

What would change my mind: any of these companies updating their policy, or evidence that one of the approaches I've questioned works better for the people it covers. If either happens, this section gets updated.

7. Bias checks: the assumptions that slip in#

Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 20 November 2026.

Who it's for: anyone writing, reviewing or running support, and especially whoever owns the policy.

Most of the ways this goes wrong aren't deliberate. They're assumptions about who's affected, what it looks like and what it means, built into a policy without anyone noticing. Run these checks on your policy, your facilities and your processes before you launch, and again whenever you review them.

Check What it looks like when it goes wrong Why it matters
Age Support aimed at people over 50, so it misses people going through perimenopause, early menopause, or menopause brought on by surgery or treatment Menopause before 40 and induced menopause are both recognised by WHO (WHO), and the best-known UK survey left early menopause out entirely (section 2)
Gender Products and bins only in the women's toilets, and a policy written only for "women" Trans and gender diverse people describe limited access to products and toilets that don't work for them (Pasmanns et al., 2026)
Who counts as typical Timelines and symptom lists taken from research on white women and treated as everyone's How long hot flushes and night sweats last differs a lot between ethnic groups in a large US study (Avis et al., 2015)
Disability A menopause or period policy that doesn't connect to your existing adjustments process, so disabled people fall between the two In the Fawcett survey, 22% of disabled women had left a job because of menopause symptoms, compared with 9% of non-disabled women (Fawcett, 2022)
Performance A disclosure quietly read as a sign that someone's ability is declining In one study, menopausal stage wasn't linked to self-rated performance or absence; working conditions were (Hardy et al., 2018)
Generalising "My colleague got through it fine, so you will too" Women warned specifically against managers generalising from other people's experiences (Hardy, Griffiths and Hunter, 2017)
Ranking Treating one condition as more serious, or more legitimate, than another How serious something is for one person can't be judged from outside. That's my view, and section 3 explains why
Disclosure-dependent support Support that only starts once someone names a condition Up to 70% of people with PCOS are undiagnosed, and endometriosis takes 4 to 12 years to diagnose on average (WHO PCOS; WHO endometriosis)
Office-only flexibility "Flexibility" that means working from home, which shift, frontline and manual workers can't use Most of the research comes from office jobs, and its authors say so (section 5)
Culture Jokes and "banter" that teach people to hide it Women named jokes and flippancy as something that got in the way (Hardy, Griffiths and Hunter, 2017)
Manager confidence Managers who feel out of their depth avoid the conversation, or leave it to someone else, so the person gets nothing Avoiding the topic entirely was one of the things women said got in the way (same study)

If your people don't work in an office#

The research is thin here, so these are my suggestions rather than tested ideas. Where working from home isn't possible, the same aims can be met in other ways: swapping shifts without a long approval process, breaks people can take when they need them rather than at set times, someone who can cover at short notice, uniforms in a looser cut or lighter fabric, and somewhere cool to step away to. The point is the same as everywhere else in this volume. Build it so people don't have to explain themselves to use it.

Training managers#

If a manager feels uncomfortable or unprepared, that's a reason to train them, not a reason for them to step back. Women in the 2017 study asked for training for managers and all staff (Hardy, Griffiths and Hunter, 2017), and a review of workplace studies found awareness programmes improved knowledge and attitudes among employees and line managers (Rodrigo et al., 2023). The evidence that it changes outcomes for the people affected is still missing, so I'd treat training as necessary but not enough on its own. Pair it with the conversations in section 5 and the checks above.

Check what actually happens#

A policy can read well and still land unevenly. Once it's running, look at who's using the support and who isn't, and whether anyone's attendance or performance record changed after they told someone. Section 9 covers how to do that without collecting more than you need.

How sure is this?#

Each row is backed by evidence that the problem exists, or by a principle set out earlier in this volume. None of them has been tested as a check, so treat the table as a prompt to look, not a test you pass or fail. The suggestions for people outside offices are my view.

What would change my mind: evidence that one of these checks doesn't matter in practice, or that something I've missed matters more. Either would change the table.

8. For you, if you're living with this#

Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 20 November 2026.

Who it's for: anyone who menstruates or is going through perimenopause or menopause, and anyone living with a related condition, diagnosed or not.

This section is written to you. It won't tell you how to manage your health. That's for you and a clinician. It's about what you can expect from work, what you can ask for, and what to do if it isn't happening. Most of it is my view, built on what people in the research said they wanted.

You don't owe anyone an explanation#

You can ask for a later start, a day working from home or a change to your workload without naming a condition. A good workplace will offer support that works without a reason. If yours asks for one, you can decide how much to share, and "a health matter I'd rather keep private" is a complete answer.

You're not unusual if you keep it to yourself. In a UK survey of 896 women, most hadn't told their line manager (Griffiths, MacLennan and Hassard, 2013). That's your call, and it doesn't make you any less entitled to support.

Choosing who to tell#

You can talk to whoever you're most comfortable with: your manager, someone in HR, or someone you trust in another team. You can share some of it and not the rest, and you can change your mind later. If something's agreed, ask for it in writing. Keep a note of the arrangement, not your symptoms, so it doesn't disappear if your manager changes.

Support doesn't have to be permanent. Women in one study pointed out that difficulties often pass, so support might only be needed for a while (Hardy, Griffiths and Hunter, 2017). It's fine to ask for something for now and review it later.

What it might sound like#

These are example scenarios, not scripts, and not a promise of how your workplace will respond. They show what a reasonable conversation can look like.

Example scenario: asking without a reason. You say: "I need a later start a couple of days a month for a health matter. I'd rather not go into it, but I can make the time up in the afternoon." A good response sounds like: "That's fine. Do you want to tell me the day, or just let me know on the morning?"

Example scenario: telling someone part of it. You say: "I've got a condition that flares up roughly once a month. On those days I can work, but not in the office." A good response sounds like: "Thanks for telling me. Let's agree you can work from home on those days without checking first. Is there anything else that would help?"

Example scenario: when something isn't working. You say: "We agreed I could take breaks when I need them, but the new rota doesn't allow for that. Can we look at it again?" A good response sounds like: "You're right, that's on us. Let's sort out cover so the arrangement still works."

If the response you get is nothing like these, that's useful information. It's not a sign you asked wrong.

You don't need a diagnosis#

You don't have to know what's causing your symptoms to ask for support at work. Many conditions take years to diagnose, and endometriosis takes 4 to 12 years on average (WHO). If you're worried about your health, talk to a doctor or another qualified health professional.

If you're trans or non-binary#

Everything in this volume should be for you, and in a workplace that's got this right, it would be. That includes products in the toilets you use, being referred to the way you want, and support that doesn't ask you to explain your body or your identity to get it.

I won't pretend every workplace is there yet. The research mostly leaves you out, and plenty of policies are written as if you don't exist. If yours does, you're still entitled to the same flexibility and privacy as anyone else, and it's fair to ask for it. If raising it with your manager doesn't feel safe, use whatever other route you have.

If it isn't working#

If you've asked and nothing has changed, or the person you'd normally go to is the problem, use the second route: HR, a People contact, or whoever your organisation names. If there's no second route, that's a gap in the policy, and it's fair to say so.

If you're a manager going through this yourself#

The support you'd give your team is yours too. Being in charge doesn't mean you have to manage without it.

Where to find reliable information#

For health information, go to clinical sources: the World Health Organization, or your national health service. Be careful with brands, influencers and anything selling a product.

If you're ever having thoughts of suicide, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away.

How sure is this?#

This section is mostly my view, built on what people in the research said they wanted from work. The example scenarios are illustrations, not tested scripts. The facts in it come from sources already used earlier in this volume.

What would change my mind: hearing from readers that something here doesn't match what helped them, or that something important is missing. If you've got something to add, the ways to tell me are in section 1.

9. Knowing whether it works#

Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 20 November 2026.

Who it's for: whoever owns the policy, and leaders who want to know it's doing something.

Measure whether the system is working, never who has a condition. Everything you need to know about whether your support works can be found without anyone telling you about their health.

Why this section is mostly my view#

There isn't much to build on. A 2023 review of workplace policies found that 24 of the 28 intervention articles it looked at said nothing about evaluation. Its authors called for policies to be designed with the people they affect, and evaluated after they're put in place (Howe et al., 2023). So what follows is how I'd do it, not a tested method.

What to look at#

Area What you're asking How to find out without collecting health data
Awareness Do people know what's available and where to go? An anonymous question in a regular survey
Ease Would people feel comfortable asking for support, and from whom? An anonymous survey. Section 10 has sample questions
Access Are products and bins in every toilet? Do shift and frontline workers have real options, not just working from home? An audit of your sites and arrangements, not of people
Speed How long does it take from someone asking to something being in place? Your process records, with no reason attached
Fairness Has anyone been flagged by attendance triggers, or started on a performance process, after asking for support? A regular check by whoever owns the policy, kept confidential

What not to collect#

  • A list of who's going through menopause or has a condition.
  • Symptom information of any kind.
  • Absence records coded by condition, unless the person has asked for that themselves.

If a group in your results is small enough that someone could be identified, don't report it. Set a minimum group size before you look at the numbers, so it's not a judgement call afterwards. Health information is treated as especially sensitive in many countries, so check locally what applies to you.

Ask the people it's for#

The best measure is whether the people living with this think it's working. Give them a way to tell you anonymously, and a way to help shape the next version if they want to. That's the review's recommendation above, and it's the idea this whole volume started from.

When to review#

Look at it again a few months after you launch, and then whenever something changes: a new site, a new way of working, or feedback that something isn't landing. A fixed schedule is fine if it suits you, but don't wait for one if you already know there's a problem.

How sure is this?#

The finding that most workplace programmes aren't evaluated comes from one review. Everything else in this section is my view, built on the principles earlier in the volume.

What would change my mind: published evaluations of workplace policies on periods or menopause that show a better way to measure what works. If they appear, this section will follow them.

10. Templates#

Version 0.1.1, draft. Section last reviewed 20 November 2026.

Who it's for: anyone putting this into practice. Copy them, change them, make them yours.

There's no policy template here on purpose. Section 6 links to three published ones that already do that job well. These four fill the gaps around a policy. They're starting points, built from earlier sections, not tested tools.

1. A one-page guide for managers#

From section 5. Share it with every manager, not just the ones who've been told something.

Before anyone says anything

  • People can talk to whoever they're comfortable with. That might not be you, and that's fine.
  • Support should work without a reason. Don't ask for one.

When someone tells you

  • Listen. Thank them. Ask what would help, and let them lead.
  • Keep it private unless they tell you otherwise. Agree what gets written down.
  • Don't assume their condition is behind everything, and don't compare them to anyone else.
  • No jokes, and no fuss. Don't make it a big issue, and don't avoid it.

Before any formal process

  • Before any attendance or performance process starts, offer support first. Ask whether anything would help, without asking what's causing it.

If someone tells you they're having thoughts of suicide

  • Take it seriously. If they're in immediate danger, don't leave them alone. Help them reach your local emergency number or a crisis line.

If you feel out of your depth

  • Ask for training or support. Don't step back and leave them with nothing.

2. A record of what's been agreed#

There's no box for a condition or a reason, on purpose. Agree with the person what goes in it and who can see it.

What's been agreed Starting Look at it again on Who can see this record

3. A facilities and access check#

From sections 4 and 7. Run it for every site, and for the people who don't work at one.

4. Anonymous survey questions#

From section 9. Add them to a survey you already run, so nobody can tell who's answering because of this. Use a simple scale, from strongly disagree to strongly agree, plus one open question.

  1. I know what support is available for periods, perimenopause, menopause and related conditions.
  2. I know who I could talk to about it, and I'd have more than one option.
  3. I'd feel comfortable asking for support, whoever I asked.
  4. I could get support without having to explain my health.
  5. When people do ask, I believe it doesn't count against them.
  6. What would make support here better? (open answer)

Report the results only for groups big enough that nobody can be identified.

How sure is this?#

These templates turn the earlier sections into something you can use. They're as strong as the sections they come from, and none has been tested on its own. If you use one and change it, I'd like to hear what you changed and why.
