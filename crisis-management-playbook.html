About This Document

Small studios, open-source maintainers, and volunteer-run communities absorb the same crises large organisations do — a bad patch, a compromised dependency, a moderation dispute that spills outward — with none of the incident-response infrastructure. There's usually no comms team, no legal on retainer, no precedent to fall back on. Just whoever's awake when it starts.

This playbook exists to be that precedent. Part 0 is action only — checklists you can execute without reading anything else. Part 1 is the modules underneath, each opening with a checklist and putting the reasoning below it. Part 2 is copy-paste templates. Parts 3–6 are the evidence base, the failure modes, three sourced case files, and a post-incident review. Nothing in Parts 3–6 is needed to act — they're there for whoever wants to know why before they trust it, or needs to defend the approach internally.

There's also a standalone, printable one-page version of Part 0 — designed to be pinned somewhere visible before you need it, not found by searching for it mid-incident.

Structure Four checklists, not one list of forty items

Chunked around the aviation-checklist research on working memory under stress — a long procedure becomes a small number of retrievable groups.

Triage Six-class classification table

Two minutes to classify a situation from Product to Governance — each class routes to a different module, a different speaker, and a different legal exposure.

Templates Eight ready-to-use response templates

First acknowledgement, holding update, technical findings, constrained silence, moderation policy, moderator brief, refund routing, decision log.

Roles Five-role command structure

Decision Maker, Comms Owner, Technical Lead, Moderation Lead, Scribe — with explicit rules for which two can never be the same person.

Evidence Built on published frameworks, not vibes

CDC's CERC model, Coombs' Situational Crisis Communication Theory, NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3, and the aviation human-factors research behind checklist design.

0.1 First 60 minutes
  1. 1Name a Decision Maker and a Comms Owner. Two different people. The Comms Owner does nothing else until this is over.
  2. 2Open a decision log. One document. Every decision gets a timestamp, a name, and a basis. Start now, not later.
  3. 3Classify the incident using the table in 0.2. Two minutes. Write the class in the log.
  4. 4Answer: is there anything a user must do right now to protect themselves? If yes, that goes at the top of your first message. If no, say so explicitly.
  5. 5Identify what you cannot legally say. Ask before you promise, not after.
  6. 6Take precautionary containment action if this is a code or access incident. Suspend access, pull the artefact, revoke the key. Frame it as precautionary, not as a verdict.
  7. 7Post the first acknowledgement. Template 2.1. You don't need answers. You need to show someone competent is awake.
  8. 8Pick one channel as the source of truth. Everything else links to it. Nothing gets restated.
  9. 9Tell staff and moderators not to discuss it publicly and give them the line to use instead. Template 2.6.
  10. 10Set the next update time and put it in the first post. Specific time. Timezone included.

0.4 Do not

Short list. These are the moves that reliably make it worse.

  • ✕Do not go silent. Silence is filled by the least charitable available theory, which then frames everything you say afterwards.
  • ✕Do not lock or purge channels. It reads as concealment and relocates the conversation somewhere you have no voice.
  • ✕Do not lead with intent. People have already judged the act. Intent arguments read as excuses.
  • ✕Do not let anyone freelance. One message, every channel, or silence until the message exists.
  • ✕Do not put junior staff in front. If it needs a face, it needs the most senior available person.
  • ✕Do not miss a deadline you set yourself. It is the only thing you are actually building.
  • ✕Do not use euphemism for a deliberate act. It will be treated as a second deception.
  • ✕Do not promise transparency you're legally unable to deliver.

Modules Each module: what to do, then why. Take only what applies.
1.1 Command structure
Do
  • ☐Name the five roles in 0.5 and tell the team who holds what
  • ☐Free the Comms Owner from all other duties for the duration
  • ☐Start the decision log — Template 2.8
  • ☐Designate one channel as the source of truth
  • ☐Send the staff line: "During this incident, don't discuss it publicly. Point people to [channel]. This isn't about trust — inconsistency is what hurts us."
Why

The Comms Owner role is the one that gets skipped and it's the one that matters most. It can't be bolted onto someone who already has a full-time crisis job, because the moment their primary job demands attention, communication stops — and communication stopping is what turns a bad week into a bad quarter. If you genuinely cannot spare a person, the Decision Maker takes comms and delegates their operational work, not the reverse.

This role separation is the standard structure in mature incident response practice. Established incident response documentation consistently separates command, technical work, communication, and record-keeping into distinct roles, precisely because one person cannot hold both the technical picture and the external narrative under load.

The decision log exists for three reasons: it prevents contradictory statements across channels, it makes the post-incident review possible, and — if any of this becomes a legal matter — it's the difference between a defensible process and a reconstruction from memory.

1.2 The first statement
Do — in this order, no reordering
  1. Instructing information. What should a user do right now? If nothing, say so explicitly.
  2. Adjusting information. Acknowledge the effect on people. Not intent — effect.
  3. What you know, stated as verified fact. Short.
  4. What you don't know, stated as open. Be specific.
  5. Next update time. Exact, with timezone.
  6. Who owns this. A name and a role.

Use Template 2.1. Do not include: intent, defensiveness, comparisons to other companies, or any promise about an outcome you don't control.

Why

The order is not stylistic. The gold-standard framework in emergency communication is the CDC's Crisis and Emergency Risk Communication (CERC) model, which sets out six principles: be first, be right, be credible, express empathy, promote action, show respect. Two of those bear directly on the order above. On being right, the guidance is to give facts in increments — tell people what you know when you know it, tell them what you don't know, and tell them if you expect to know more later. On credibility, the guidance is blunt: tell the truth, don't withhold to avoid embarrassment, and recognise that uncertainty is worse than bad news, because rumour does more damage than hard truth.

The action-first ordering has the same evidential basis. Giving people something specific to do restores a sense of control in circumstances that feel out of control — which is why instructing information outranks everything, including the apology.

The same conclusion arrives from the crisis communications literature by a different route. Coombs' Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT) is the most replicated framework in the field, and one of the most common misapplications of it is skipping what he calls the ethical base response — instructing and adjusting information — and jumping straight to reputational strategy. That base response comes first in any crisis regardless of type.

Translated

Tell people what to do and acknowledge the harm before you say anything about yourselves. Any response that opens with an explanation of the company's intentions has already lost.

On timing

For an inbound vulnerability report, the international standard sets meaningful acknowledgement at within seven calendar days. That's the floor for a private report, not a benchmark for a public incident already in progress. For a live incident, your window is measured in hours.

1.3 Cadence and channel discipline
Do
  • ☐Publish a cadence — 12 hours for active security, 24–48 for slower matters
  • ☐Hold it without exception, including when there's nothing to report
  • ☐One channel is the source of truth; everywhere else links, never restates
  • ☐No staff member comments independently, including in DMs and side channels
  • ☐Every channel carries the identical message or stays silent until the message exists
Why

A published "no change, next update Thursday 18:00" costs nothing and buys real credibility, because it proves the channel is alive. Missing a self-set deadline destroys the only asset you're building.

The restatement rule matters more than it sounds. The moment the same information exists in three slightly different forms, the differences become the story. A well-meaning developer answering honestly in a side channel — with detail the official statement doesn't contain — will be screenshotted within four minutes and reframed as a leak or a contradiction.

Case in point — Helldivers 2 / PSN account linking (2024)

The clearest recent demonstration of what happens without this discipline: the game's own community-facing staff publicly encouraged players to leave negative reviews and file refund requests to pressure the publisher. The commercial consequence was severe — the title accumulated over 369,000 negative reviews at peak and briefly became the most negatively reviewed paid game on the platform. That's not a story about a bad community manager. It's a story about a team with no comms owner, no agreed message, and a staff member left to improvise a position on a decision they disagreed with and did not control. Give people a script and they don't have to invent one.

1.4 Moderation under load — Class F
Do
  • ☐Keep channels open. Consolidate into one pinned thread per topic instead.
  • ☐Publish the moderation rules for this incident before enforcing them — Template 2.5
  • ☐Remove only: personal information, threats and incitement, content targeting family or uninvolved third parties
  • ☐Log every removal with the rule invoked
  • ☐Never remove a post a reasonable observer would call fair criticism
  • ☐Name and defend the second-order victims explicitly and early
  • ☐Protect the individual at the centre from pile-on, whatever they did
Why

Locking is the most common instinct and the most reliably damaging one. It doesn't stop the conversation — it moves it somewhere you have no presence, no context, and no right of reply, and it confirms the concealment theory for everyone watching.

Consolidation achieves the volume control that locking was meant to achieve, is visibly not censorship, and is easy to defend. The removal log is your answer when you're accused of censorship anyway, which you will be. The credibility of the entire response rests on never removing fair criticism — one wrongly deleted post, screenshotted, undoes a week of good handling.

Second-order victims

The people this playbook exists to protect and the ones teams routinely forget: the third party falsely blamed, the uninvolved contributor caught in a boycott, the moderator accused of complicity. Naming and defending them is cheap, correct, and one of the very few actions in a crisis that generates goodwill rather than merely limiting loss.

Protecting the person at the centre is not tension with accountability

Doxxing, threats, contacting family or employers, and harassment of uninvolved people who share a name all get removed and logged — regardless of how justified the underlying grievance is. State it as policy, not as a defence of the person.

1.5 Security and supply chain — Class B
Do
  • ☐Contain first: suspend access, pull the artefact, revoke keys. Precautionary, not punitive.
  • ☐Establish and publish, in this order:
    • Affected artefacts and exact version ranges, with availability dates
    • Observed behaviour and trigger conditions
    • Scope of impact — explicitly state whether anything beyond the observed behaviour occurred: files, network calls, credentials, persistence. If nothing, say so. If unknown, give a date for when you'll know.
    • Detection and timeline: introduced, first observable, reported, confirmed, contained
    • Remediation done and user action required
    • Audit scope, reviewer, and publication date
  • ☐Commit to publishing the audit result before you know what it says
  • ☐Use third-party review if you can afford it
  • ☐Never call a deliberate act a bug, an oversight, or a compatibility issue

Use Template 2.3.

Why

The community's real question is not who did this or whether they'll be punished. It's "is the code on my machine safe right now." Answer that and you buy the time to handle everything else properly.

The single most consequential thing you will publish

Crash-only versus data-touching. Users cannot assess their own risk until you state it, and until they can, every other message you send is noise.

Run it as an incident, not as PR. The current baseline reference is NIST SP 800-61 Revision 3 (April 2025), which superseded the long-standing Revision 2 handling guide entirely and reorganised incident response around the six functions of the Cybersecurity Framework 2.0. You're not going to implement a NIST profile mid-crisis, but it gives you defensible structure that wasn't invented on the spot. One of its useful shifts is treating improvement as continuous throughout the incident rather than as an end-of-incident retrospective — if you can fix something now, fix it now.

Self-attestation is worth very little to an audience that has just discovered it was deceived by someone inside the tent. External review with published scope converts "trust us" into "trust the method."

On the "it only targeted bad actors" defence

This surfaces in essentially every incident of this kind, and it survives neither ethical nor technical scrutiny. The clearest documented parallel is the 2022 node-ipc case, where a maintainer of a widely-depended-upon package shipped code that damaged files on machines geolocated to particular countries, framed by its author as protest rather than malware. Security analysts described it as destructive corruption of files by a maintainer, along with attempts to conceal and restate the sabotage in different forms, and treated the protest motivation as irrelevant to the classification. It followed the colors and faker sabotage weeks earlier and prompted direct questions about whether that maintainer should retain publishing rights at all.

The transferable point: the ecosystem judged the act, not the intent, and the trust consequence attached to the person's future access rather than to the specific artefact. Any statement leading with intent is arguing a case the audience has already closed.

1.6 Individual conduct — Class D
Do
  • ☐Say plainly that you cannot discuss an individual's employment, and explain why — Template 2.4
  • ☐Move every commitment you can onto the non-personnel track: access controls, review requirements, audit scope and date, policy change, vetting change
  • ☐Separate historic conduct (for investigation) from present conduct (cooperation, remediation, honesty — observable now)
  • ☐Run a proper process: investigate, put the allegations, hear them, decide, document
  • ☐Decide internally, early: if the outcome is that they stay, can you defend it publicly with reasons, and will they say something themselves?
Why

This is the module where good instincts do the most damage, because the transparency reflex that serves you in Class B is actively harmful here. You cannot discuss an individual's employment status or process publicly while it's running. That's not corporate evasiveness — it's a constraint that exists in most jurisdictions partly to protect the accused's right to a fair process, which is worth defending even when it's inconvenient.

The move that costs nothing and changes everything

Explaining the silence. "We're not commenting" reads as protection. "We can't discuss any individual's employment while a process is running, that applies whatever the outcome turns out to be, and it exists to make the process fair — here's what we can talk about" reads as procedure.

Then convert "trust this person" into "you don't have to trust any individual, including us." A community demanding someone's removal is usually, underneath, demanding a guarantee. Removal is one way to provide it. Structural control is a better one, because it survives the next person and it doesn't depend on an outcome you can't promise.

If the honest answer to the last checkbox is no, the outcome is already decided and the only remaining variable is how many weeks you spend discovering it. Negotiated exits exist for exactly this configuration and are almost always reached later than they should be.

1.7 Governance failure — Class E
Do
  • ☐Disclose it yourselves, first, in full, before anyone else finds it
  • ☐All four parts: what was known, when and by whom; what the reasoning was; why it was wrong; what has structurally changed
  • ☐The principal speaks, in their own voice, by name
  • ☐Do not moderate the rumour — answer it with dated specifics or accept that it becomes permanent
Why

This is the most damaging class and it has the fewest options. A self-disclosed governance failure is a serious problem. An exposed one is a terminal credibility event, because every prior statement you made retroactively becomes a lie.

Three parts out of four reads as spin

All four or don't bother.

Unfalsifiable rumours are a governance problem, not a moderation problem. When a community starts theorising that leadership knew, silence cannot disprove it and moderating it away confirms it. The only thing that touches it is a direct, dated, specific factual statement from the principal. If you can't make that statement, understand clearly that the theory is now permanent.

The named-human effect — Helldivers 2

An earlier formal acknowledgement that simply redirected players to publisher support failed to slow the reviews at all. What began the recovery was the studio CEO publicly accepting that the backlash was warranted and apologising for how it had unfolded. The formal redirect failed. The named person accepting the criticism as legitimate worked.

1.8 Commercial decision backlash — Class C
Do
  • ☐Decide within 24 hours, honestly: would you reverse this under enough pressure?
  • ☐If yes — reverse now, not in week four
  • ☐If genuinely no — name the actual constraint and stop defending the decision on its merits
  • ☐Do not argue that the change was in the terms
Why

Class C is the only class where reversal is on the table, which changes the entire calculus. The most common error is treating a reversible decision as a matter of principle.

A reversal in week one is a company listening to its players. The identical reversal in week four is a company that had to be forced — you pay the full reputational price for a decision you ended up unmaking anyway.

The evidence that reversal works is strong — Helldivers 2

The publisher dropped the account-linking requirement within days of the peak. Players then reversed their own reviews at scale — over 200,000 positive reviews in a single day — and the title moved off the most-negatively-reviewed position it had briefly held. The damage was almost entirely recoverable because the underlying decision was.

"We are legally entitled to do this" is true and irrelevant. The community is not making a legal claim. "We don't have a choice and here's why" is survivable. "This is actually good for you" is not.

1.9 Refunds and commerce
Do
  • ☐Publish refund routing on day one — Template 2.7
  • ☐Honour your own published policy at its most generous reading
  • ☐Process fast, no case-by-case argument
  • ☐Track daily volume as your primary sentiment metric
  • ☐Get legal advice on your specific policy wording — do not resolve it yourself under pressure
Why

The refund exposure is almost always small. The reputational cost of appearing to renege is not. A refusal becomes the story; a refund is a transaction.

Routing is the cheap win. If most sales run through third-party storefronts, those platforms' policies govern those purchases, and a large slice of your inbound anger will be from people who believe they're being stonewalled when they're actually in the wrong queue. One paragraph removes a whole category of grievance.

Discretionary language is still a representation. Wording along the lines of you can ask outside the normal limits and we'll take a look is a promise to consider, not an unconditional guarantee — but it creates an expectation, and the gap between the expectation and your behaviour is what gets screenshotted. Departing from your own published commitments once customers have relied on them also raises unfair-practice questions under UK and EU consumer regimes.

You do not control the refund perimeter. In sufficiently visible incidents, storefronts have extended eligibility well beyond their normal windows independently of the developer — Valve did exactly that during the Helldivers episode.

Refunds beat vibes as a metric. Discord temperature measures the loudest twenty people. Refunds measure decisions.

1.10 Your people
Do
  • ☐Brief volunteer moderators before the public statement — Template 2.6
  • ☐Give them permitted lines and explicit permission to say "I don't have that information"
  • ☐Tell them they can step back at any point with no consequence
  • ☐Give them a private channel to a named staff member
  • ☐Rotate anyone in the mentions every four hours
  • ☐Keep junior staff off the front line
Why

Volunteer moderators are the most exposed and least supported people in the entire event. They're unpaid, publicly identified, and absorbing abuse for decisions they didn't make and often weren't told about.

Rotation is a welfare measure and also a quality measure — judgement degrades fast under sustained hostility. Putting a community manager in front of a decision they didn't make is both a welfare failure and a strategic one, because the audience knows they lack authority and reads the substitution as evasion.

Your duty of care to the individual at the centre does not lapse either. A serious harassment outcome becomes its own crisis on top of the one you already have.

Why this works Not needed to act. Useful when deciding whether to trust it, adapting it, or arguing for it internally.
3.1 The communication principles underneath

The gold-standard framework in emergency communication is the CDC's Crisis and Emergency Risk Communication model, developed for public health emergencies and widely adopted well beyond them. It sets out six principles: be first, be right, be credible, express empathy, promote action, show respect.

The specific guidance behind three of those directly shapes Part 0 and module 1.2:

  • Be first — if the information is yours to give, give it as soon as possible; if you can't, explain how you're working to get it. First sources become the standard everything else is measured against.
  • Be right — give facts in increments. Say what you know when you know it, say what you don't know, and say when you expect to know more.
  • Be credible — tell the truth, don't withhold to avoid embarrassment, and understand that uncertainty is worse than bad news because rumour does more damage than hard truth.
Promote action

Why instructing information sits above the apology: giving people something specific to do restores a sense of control in circumstances that feel out of control.

CERC also defines five lifecycle stages — pre-crisis, initial, maintenance, resolution, evaluation. Part 0 covers initial, Part 1 covers maintenance, Part 6 covers evaluation, and the last instruction in Part 6 is to build your own pre-crisis stage.

3.2 Why the class matters more than the severity

Coombs' Situational Crisis Communication Theory is the most heavily replicated framework in crisis communications. Its core claim is that stakeholders assign responsibility before they assign anger, and that response strategy must be matched to the level of attributed responsibility. It groups crises into victim, accidental, and intentional clusters, and responses into deny, diminish, and rebuild families, with crisis type, crisis history, and prior reputation together predicting how audiences will attribute blame.

Two findings are load-bearing for this playbook:

Preventable crises damage reputation more than any other type

Rebuild strategies — apology and compensation — produce the strongest recovery for them. Almost everything in classes C, D, and E is a preventable crisis. Deny and diminish are the wrong tools and will read as contempt.

The ethical base response comes first regardless of type

Skipping instructing and adjusting information in favour of reputational strategy is one of the most common misapplications of the framework.

3.3 Same outcome, different route

Several classes end in the same visible outcome — a statement, an audit, a policy change, sometimes a departure. They cannot be run the same way, and pretending otherwise is how teams make promises in week one they have to break in week three.

Speech constraints differ. Class B: publish facts as fast as you can verify them. Class D: you are legally constrained and will remain so for the whole process. Applying Class B instincts to Class D prejudices the process and creates liability. Applying Class D caution to Class B looks like a cover-up.

Timelines differ and conflict. Security remediation runs in hours. Employment process runs in weeks and cannot be compressed without exposure. Community patience runs to about forty-eight hours. Split them publicly and explain that you're splitting them, or the slow track poisons the fast one.

Reversibility differs. Class C decisions can be reversed and often should be. Class B and D facts can only be remediated. Teams frequently try to reverse their way out of a Class B by shipping a fix and saying nothing, which fails because the trust question was never about the code.

The evidentiary bar differs. An unverified technical claim costs you a correction. An unverified claim about a named individual costs you a defamation exposure.

Who can credibly speak differs. Class C and E require the principal. No community manager can carry a governance failure, and asking them to is unfair and ineffective in equal measure.

3.4 Why this document is shaped the way it is

The structure is not arbitrary. It follows what the human factors literature established about procedural documents used under stress — most of it developed for aviation checklists, and explicitly intended by its authors to transfer to other high-risk domains.

Short-term memory is the binding constraint

Degani and Wiener's foundational work on flight-deck checklists identifies limited working memory as one of the greatest constraints on human performance, and notes that many procedural documents place unrealistic demands on it. The mitigation they describe is chunking — grouping items into defined units around a common factor, so a long procedure becomes a small number of retrievable groups rather than one undifferentiated list. That's why Part 0 is four short checklists rather than one list of thirty items, and why the modules are separate rather than continuous.

Their other design recommendations that this document tries to honour: restricted vocabulary, frequently used words, sequential ordering that matches the order of actual operation, and clear allocation of duties between people rather than to "the team."

Findability beats completeness. The prevailing view in incident response practice is that a runbook nobody can find during an incident may as well not exist, and that conflicting documents breed hesitation — each procedure needs one definitive source so responders know which document to trust under pressure. Hence: one file, action at the front, no cross-referencing you have to chase, and a single source-of-truth rule applied to your own channels as well.

Start simple and add granularity later. Mature incident response guidance recommends not worrying about severity tiers at the beginning — just establish whether something is an incident, get the roles and the trigger working, and add sophistication once the basic process runs. This playbook deliberately has no severity matrix for the same reason. If you get to the point of needing one, you've outgrown this document, which is the correct outcome.

05
Case files Short, sourced, each isolating one transferable mechanism.
Helldivers 2 / PSN account linking (2024)
C · Commercial

A publisher-mandated account-linking requirement triggered one of the largest review-bombing events in the platform's history, peaking above 369,000 negative reviews, with community staff publicly directing players toward reviews and refunds, and the title pulled from sale in a large number of territories where the required accounts weren't available. The publisher reversed within days, the studio CEO publicly accepted the criticism as warranted and apologised, and players reversed their reviews at scale — over 200,000 positive reviews in a single day.

Mechanism: reversal works, and works fast, when the decision is reversible. Absence of comms discipline turns internal disagreement into public escalation.

node-ipc / peacenotwar (2022)
B · Security

A maintainer of a widely-depended-upon package shipped code causing file damage on machines in particular locations, framed by its author as protest. Security analysis described it as destructive corruption of files by a maintainer with attempts to conceal and restate the sabotage in different forms, treating the protest motivation as irrelevant to classification. It followed the colors and faker sabotage weeks earlier and prompted direct questions about whether the maintainer should retain publishing privileges.

Mechanism: ecosystems judge the act, not the stated target or motive, and the trust consequence attaches to the person's future access rather than to the artefact.

Package registry incidents, generally
B · Security

The pattern is consistent: a maintainer account or artefact is compromised or sabotaged, a version ships, and the damage is done before flagging because automated systems pull dependencies continuously. Dormant or lightly maintained packages retain broad ecosystem reach long after active development stops, so a recovered or hijacked maintainer identity can reach a large dependency graph without ever touching source control.

Mechanism: in a modding or plugin ecosystem your exposure is not your own code. It's every widely-installed community artefact and every account that can publish one. Ecosystem-level controls are the durable fix; individual accountability is not.

After Run this within two weeks of the incident closing, while people still remember.
  • ☐When could we first have known, and when did we actually know? The gap is your detection problem.
  • ☐What did we say that we later had to correct? Why did we say it?
  • ☐Which commitments did we hit, and which did we miss? Missed ones are the durable damage.
  • ☐Where did approval bottleneck?
  • ☐Who absorbed the worst of it, and what did we do for them?
  • ☐What structural control would have prevented this — and is it now in place, with a named owner and a date?
Then write your own two-page version of this

Your product, your channels, your team, names filled in. Store it somewhere findable at 2am. Review it every six months. It takes an afternoon and it's the highest-return preparation available to a small team.

The current NIST guidance makes this point structurally: improvement is continuous through the incident lifecycle, not reserved for a retrospective at the end. If there's something you can fix now, fix it now.
